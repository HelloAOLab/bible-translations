## Overview

For generations, English Bible translations have quietly imported assumptions foreign to the biblical authors: Greek philosophical categories, medieval theological vocabulary, and modernizing tendencies that flatten the ancient world into something more familiar and less strange. The result is a Bible that feels accessible but often obscures what the text actually says.

The Accessible Ancients Bible aims to push back against this, but within limits. Accessibility is not a concession; it is the design goal. The AAB is calibrated to take readers farther into the ancient world than any widely-used modern translation, while remaining recognizable enough that they do not feel they have picked up a foreign text. Most readers have never been invited to consider what the biblical authors actually meant by firmament, Sheol, the divine council, or the Accuser. This translation makes that invitation without requiring readers to already know Ancient Near Eastern backgrounds to receive it. The cost of this accessibility is deliberate: some renderings stop short of where a fully foreignizing translation would go. That is by design. For those who want the biblical text rendered with maximum fidelity to its ancient context, we are developing the Ancient Voice Translation (AVT), a sister project that prioritizes accuracy over familiarity and makes no concessions to what readers expect. The AAB is the on-ramp; the AVT is the road.

This translation prioritizes readability while making targeted corrections where traditional renderings obscure the original meaning. We believe accuracy and accessibility can coexist, even if tensions remain.

**Our Commitments:** We affirm the Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy (1978) in full. We affirm *sola scriptura*: the Old and New Testaments (the 66-book Protestant canon[^1]) are the sole infallible authority for faith and practice.[^2] These are not peripheral convictions; they are the foundation on which this work stands.

**Audience:** General readers, ministry contexts, church use. People who want accuracy but don't want to feel like they're reading a different Bible. We preserved the familiar feel of modern translations while making surgical corrections where it matters most.

**On the Autographic Text:** The CSBI affirms that inspiration applies to "the autographic text of Scripture, which in the providence of God can be ascertained from available manuscripts" (Article X). We understand "autographic text" to encompass the entire process by which God gave Scripture to his people: original composition, editorial arrangement, and transmission, all under divine superintendence. Scripture itself evidences editorial activity (Deut 34; Prov 25:1; Jer 36), and we regard such work as part of the inspired canonical process. The biblical authors were clearly unbothered by this; we should not import anxieties they did not share. The "autograph" is the text as God intended his covenant community to receive it.

**Textual Evidence:** As new historical data emerges and older manuscript variants are discovered, we incorporate the best available evidence into our ongoing work. The Dead Sea Scrolls, the Septuagint, and other ancient witnesses provide textual data that scholarship continues to explore. We do not assume conflict where none has been demonstrated. Where text-critical study points to an earlier or superior reading, we follow the evidence. This does not undermine inspiration; it reflects humility before what God was doing. The canon is complete. Our task is faithful reception. This translation represents our best current effort; we will issue updates as our understanding improves, documenting what changed and why.

**Use of Extra-Biblical Sources:** We consult the Apocrypha, pseudepigrapha (such as 1 Enoch), and wider Ancient Near Eastern literature, not as authoritative Scripture, but as essential background for determining semantic range and understanding what the original audience would have heard. When Isaiah uses imagery that echoes Canaanite mythology, or when Jude alludes to 1 Enoch, knowing those sources illuminates the biblical text. We use them as context, not canon.

**Public Domain:** This translation is released into the public domain. We want churches, ministries, and developers to use it freely without licensing restrictions or fees.

## A Word to Readers: Translation Is Not Commentary

This translation will unsettle some readers. Terms you expect to see have been replaced with terms that may feel unfamiliar or even wrong. Before you conclude that we have abandoned orthodoxy, we ask you to consider a distinction that is easy to forget: **translation and theology are different tasks.**

**Translation asks:** What did the authors write? What do the Hebrew and Greek words mean? How can we render them faithfully in English?

**Theology asks:** What does this mean? How does it fit with the rest of Scripture? What doctrines does it support or challenge?

These are both legitimate tasks. But they must not be confused. When readers expect a translation to *confirm* their theological system, they are asking translators to do the work of commentators. And when translators comply, when they render texts in ways designed to support particular doctrines rather than to represent what the source languages say, they are no longer translating. They are interpreting, and hiding that interpretation behind the authority of "the Bible says."

**We refuse to do this.**

If you believe in penal substitutionary atonement, or the immortality of the soul, or any other doctrine held dear, you may argue for it from Scripture. But you may not ask us to translate Hebrew and Greek words in ways that import theological categories foreign to the original authors, simply because those categories feel more supportive of your doctrine. The doctrine must be argued from what the text *actually says*, not from what a translator has made it say.

**This principle cuts in all directions.** We are not translating to support *our* theological preferences either. We are translating because the words mean what they mean, and the reader deserves to see what the words mean.

**The question you should ask is simple:** Are we being faithful to the source languages in an intellectually honest way?

If yes, then we have done our job. You may disagree with our interpretive conclusions. You may think the theology we derive from our translation is wrong. Fine. Make that argument. But make it as an argument about interpretation, not as an accusation that we have mistranslated.

If no, if we have rendered a Hebrew or Greek word inaccurately, if we have missed a semantic nuance, if we have imposed a meaning the source language does not support, then by all means, correct us. We welcome that feedback. That is the conversation we want to have.

But do not ask us to make the text say what it does not say.

When Moses approached the burning bush, God said: "Take off your sandals, for the place where you are standing is holy ground." We would say to readers: take off your systematic theology for a moment. Take off your creedal commitments, your tradition, your assumptions about what the Bible *must* say. Stand before the text barefoot. See what it actually says. You may find it says what you expected. You may find it says something richer, stranger, more unsettling. Either way, you will be encountering Scripture rather than a translation shaped to confirm what you already believe.

The doctrines you hold dear are not threatened by honest translation. If they are true, they can be argued from what the text actually says. If they require a translation that hides or distorts the source languages, then the problem is not with the translation.

We believe the reader deserves the text, not a translator's theology dressed up as the text.

### A Compromising Translation (By Design)

We must also acknowledge a tension. We have said we will do our best to bring forward what the text actually says, especially in clear cases where theological clarification is at stake. But we are *not* doing the full breadth of changes that could be done to bring forward the world of the biblical authors as much as we could.

For example, we will not change "Jesus" to "Joshua," even though this would be more faithful to what the original audience heard. The name Ἰησοῦς (Iēsous) is the Greek form of the Hebrew יְהוֹשֻׁעַ (Yehoshua), the same name as the Israelite leader who succeeded Moses and led God's people into the promised land. The theological resonance is profound: Jesus (Joshua) is leading God's people into the promised land of God's kingdom, just as the first Joshua led Israel into Canaan. But changing the name would so disorient modern readers that they would struggle to read the text at all.

This is a compromising translation. We push things as far as we think we can without putting off modern audiences so much they will not read it. We foreignize where theological meaning is at stake (firmament, Sheol, purification offering, the Accuser). We domesticate where only familiarity is at stake (Jesus, Jerusalem, Moses). There are cases where a more accurate rendering exists but would be too disorienting to serve this translation's audience \-- "Gehenna" instead of "hell" is one such case, and the rationale entry for that term explains why the AAB retains "hell" while the AVT will not. We flag these decisions where they arise rather than pretending they do not exist.

For a more uncompromising and therefore more foreignizing work, we are developing the **Ancient Voice Translation (AVT)**. The AVT will use Yahweh instead of LORD, Yeshua instead of Jesus, measured parataxis to preserve Hebrew narrative rhythm, and stricter root consistency (Leitwort fidelity). It will feel stranger. It will also let readers encounter the text with fewer layers of cultural translation between them and the original. The AAB and AVT share the same textual and theological foundation; they serve different readers with different tolerances for strangeness.

### Why Public Domain Matters

**This translation is public domain and will remain so forever.**

We are releasing the Accessible Ancients Bible under a CC0 public domain dedication because we believe Scripture belongs to everyone. There are no licensing fees, no restrictions, no permissions needed. Use it in apps, study tools, church bulletins, academic papers, commercial products, or anything else. Fork it. Improve it. Build on it. This is a permanent, irrevocable commitment.

Why does this matter? Because access to Scripture is hindered by barriers most readers never see.

**The landscape before the BSB:**

Until the Berean Standard Bible entered the public domain, there was no quality modern English translation that anyone could freely use. Consider the options:

- **KJV (1611):** Public domain, but archaic. "Thee," "thou," "wherefore," "peradventure." Useful for study, difficult for modern tools and audiences.  
- **ASV (1901):** Public domain, also archaic. Slightly more literal than the KJV but similarly dated.  
- **WEB (World English Bible):** Public domain and modern, but not widely respected or adopted. Few scholars recommend it; few churches use it.  
- **Every respected modern translation** (ESV, NIV, NASB, CSB, NLT, NKJV, NET) **is copyrighted.**

**What this means in practice:**

- Want to build a Bible app? License a translation (often expensive) or use the KJV.  
- Want to create a Scripture-integrated study tool? Permission required.  
- Want to make a video with Scripture on screen? Check the license.  
- Want to print a booklet with extended Scripture passages? Most licenses cap at 500 verses before requiring written permission.  
- Want to create a derivative work, a simplified version, a new translation into another language using English as a base? Forbidden by most licenses.

Most Christians never encounter this barrier because they never try to *build* anything with Scripture. They read, they study, they quote. The moment you try to create something new, you hit a wall. And the wall is invisible until you hit it.

The BSB changed everything. Finally, a modern, readable, scholarly translation that anyone could use without permission. The AAB builds on that foundation, correcting specific terminology and textual decisions while preserving the BSB's readability and public domain status.

Other translations are publicly available for personal use but require those who want to incorporate them into tools, apps, or publications to (a) know the translation exists, (b) find out who controls the rights, and (c) request permission. These steps are not difficult for large organizations with legal departments. They are insurmountable barriers for the small developer building a Bible app in her spare time, the pastor in a developing nation who wants to print study materials, or the missionary who needs a translation he can freely modify for a new context.

Our goal is simple: a quality public domain translation that anyone can use, available through the **Free Use Bible API** (bible.helloao.org). No permissions. No forms. No waiting. Just Scripture, free as it should be.

### Our Aspiration

We are not hedging. We want this to be the best modern English translation available.

That is an audacious claim for an early public draft from a small team. We know. We do not even recommend this translation ourselves yet. We are publishing it to invite the scrutiny and collaboration necessary to get there. This is the starting point of a journey, not a finished product.

Candidly: if the ESV were public domain, we would have started this work from that base. Its prose is often polished, and many of its textual decisions are sound. But it is copyrighted, and Crossway controls who can use it and how. The BSB is free. That freedom, combined with our conviction that we can do better on the issues that matter most, is why we are here.

### The Problem with Modern Translations

This is not an attack on the ESV. It is a critique of a systemic problem across modern English translations.

The ESV, NIV, NASB, CSB, NLT, and other major translations share common weaknesses:

**Failure to incorporate post-1947 discoveries.** The Dead Sea Scrolls (1947-1956) provide Hebrew manuscripts 1,000 years older than the Masoretic Text. The Ugaritic texts (discovered 1929\) illuminate divine council theology, Canaanite religious vocabulary, and Hebrew poetic forms. Yet most translations still default to the MT even where better readings exist, still sanitize cosmic geography, and still flatten the supernatural worldview of the biblical authors.

**Theological tradition trumping linguistics.** Translations inherit categories from centuries of Christian interpretation and impose them on texts that predate those categories. Nephesh becomes "soul" (importing Greek dualism). Chattat becomes "sin offering" (importing penal substitution). Raqia becomes "expanse" (avoiding the solid dome the Hebrew describes). The source languages say what they say; translations should let them say it.

**Internal inconsistency.** The same Hebrew or Greek word is translated differently across passages without clear justification. Sheol becomes "Sheol" here and "grave" there. Hades becomes "Hades" in Acts but "hell" in Luke. Abaddon is transliterated in Revelation but translated as "Destruction" in Job. These inconsistencies hide textual connections and confuse readers.

**The KJV shadow.** Many translation choices are inherited from the King James tradition without re-examination. Ironically, the KJV translators were often more literal than their modern successors. They kept "firmament" because that is what the Hebrew means. We speculate that modern translators likely change it out of a desire to be culturally sensitive, but this obscures the semantic range of the Hebrew and erases connections biblical authors make elsewhere (e.g., Ezek 1:22-26, where the prophet sees the *raqia* as a solid surface upon which Yahweh's throne rests; Job 37:18, which describes the skies as "hard as a cast metal mirror").

**Committee conservatism and market pressure.** Translation committees are large, theologically diverse, and funded by publishers who need to sell Bibles. This creates structural incentives toward consensus over accuracy, continuity over correction, and familiarity over fidelity. The result: translations that are cautious, internally inconsistent, and resistant to incorporating scholarly advances that might unsettle readers.

The ESV has become the default "serious" Bible in many evangelical circles. We believe this status is unearned. The ESV is a decent translation, but it has clear internal consistency problems, makes theologically loaded choices while presenting itself as neutral, and obscures source-language meaning in ways that are widely acknowledged by scholars but rarely discussed in churches. We are not saying the ESV is bad. We are saying it does not deserve its reputation as the gold standard, and we want to offer an alternative that takes the problems seriously.

The AAB is an attempt to do what all modern translations should have done but have not: incorporate the best available scholarship, prioritize source-language meaning over theological comfort, maintain internal consistency, and be honest about what the texts actually say.

We are not claiming perfection. We are claiming that these problems are real, widely acknowledged by scholars, and largely ignored by major translations. Someone needs to address them. We are trying.

### A Call to Scholars and Teachers

The ESV achieved wide adoption in part because respected scholars and teachers endorsed it. Names like Wayne Grudem, J.I. Packer, and R.C. Sproul lent credibility that helped the translation gain traction in churches, seminaries, and publishing.

We are asking for similar engagement, not as endorsement of conclusions you may not share, but as honest evaluation of our work.

**If you are a biblical scholar:** How have we done? Have we rendered the Hebrew and Greek accurately? Where have we erred? We are not asking you to agree with every decision, only to tell us whether our decisions fall within the range of legitimate translation. If they do not, show us where and why, and we will fix it.

**If you are a pastor or teacher:** Does this translation serve your people? Can you teach from it? Are there places where our choices create unnecessary confusion or undermine clarity? Tell us.

**If you are willing to go further:** What would it take for you to recommend this translation for its stated goals? We are not asking anyone to abandon their preferred Bible. We are asking whether the AAB can join the list of translations you consider acceptable for serious use. If not, what would need to change?

We cannot improve without critical feedback. We cannot gain traction without credible voices willing to say, "This translation is doing something legitimate, even where I might make different choices." And we cannot serve the church if scholars who see our errors remain silent.

The ESV took years and many revisions to reach its current form. We expect the same. Help us get there faster. See [How to Add Your Voice](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1nDnDNr5jyD2FIPS4AgbP8J4NDM-vPq-BhrAcn4Qlg1I/edit#how-to-add-your-voice).

## What We Changed and What We Didn't

The AAB makes selective foreignizing choices. We preserve unfamiliar terms where doing so protects theologically significant distinctions (firmament, Sheol, the Accuser, purification offering). But we deliberately avoid foreignizing in other areas where the cost to readability outweighs the benefit.

**This is intentional, not inconsistent.** The goal is a Bible modern readers can pick up and read without constant confusion, while still encountering the text's actual worldview where it matters most.

### What We Preserved (Foreignizing)

| Category | Choice | Rationale |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Cosmology | "firmament" not "expanse" | Preserves what Hebrew actually says about the sky |
| Underworld | Sheol, Hades, Gehenna, Tartarus, Abyss | Biblical authors distinguished these; collapsing to "hell" loses geography |
| Entities | Rephaim, Azazel, Lilith, Abaddon | Proper names should be transliterated, not translated away |
| Divine council | "the Accuser" (with article), "sons of God," "holy ones" | Grammar and worldview require it |
| Sacrificial system | "purification offering," "restitution offering," "atonement cover" | Milgrom consensus; traditional terms import wrong theology |
| Messianic vocabulary | "seed" not "offspring" | Preserves Christological connections (Gen 3:15 → Gal 3:16) |
| Nephesh/psychē (targeted) | "being," "life," "one who" where clear; "soul" with footnotes elsewhere | Hebrew nephesh is not Platonic "soul"; de-Platonizing where it misleads |

### What We Did Not Change (Domesticating)

| Category | AAB Choice | AVT Will Do | Why AAB Held Back |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Divine name | "LORD" for יהוה | "Yahweh" | Readers expect "LORD"; change is jarring without preparation |
| Personal names | Moses, Jesus, Isaiah | Mosheh, Yeshua, Yeshayahu | Familiar names aid recognition; foreignizing here confuses more than it clarifies |
| Place names | Jerusalem, Egypt, Babylon | Yerushalayim, Mitsrayim, Bavel | Same reasoning; these are established English forms |
| Sentence rhythm | Normal English flow | Measured parataxis ("and... and... and...") | Hebrew's additive style feels childish in English without careful handling |
| Root consistency | Contextual variation | Leitwort fidelity (same root → same English word) | Rigid consistency often produces awkward English |
| Idioms | Smoothed where needed | Literal body-part idioms preserved | "His nostrils flared" works; "he burned in his nose" doesn't |
| Emotional seat | "heart" for emotions | "guts," "kidneys," "bowels" where Hebrew has them | Hebrew places emotions in the intestines (מֵעִים) and kidneys (כִּלְיוֹת); English readers expect "heart" |
| Measurements | Conventional handling | Cubits, ephahs, etc. with footnotes | Conversion aids comprehension without theological cost |

### The Principle

We foreignized where **theological meaning is at stake**. We domesticated where **only style or familiarity is at stake**.

"Firmament" protects a cosmological distinction the biblical authors made. "Yahweh" versus "LORD" does not change meaning; it changes feel. Both are legitimate translation choices, but they serve different purposes. The AAB prioritizes meaning-critical foreignization while keeping the reading experience accessible.

The Ancient Voice Translation will make the opposite tradeoff: full foreignization for readers who want to encounter the text as maximally strange and ancient. Both translations serve the same Scripture; they serve different readers.

## A Note on Greek Dualism and the Biblical Worldview

**A note on this section's scope:** What follows makes a case for how the biblical authors understood human nature. It is translation rationale, not systematic theology. The AAB moves readings toward the ancient Hebraic worldview on these questions \-- partially, not fully. Where a fully consistent rendering would be accurate but highly disorienting (such as replacing "soul" with "throat" in poetic contexts where the idiomatic force would be lost), the AAB retains the more familiar rendering with a footnote. The goal is steps toward the ancient world, not arrival. Readers who want maximum foreignization should consult the AVT.

One of the most pervasive interpretive lenses imposed on Scripture is Greek philosophical dualism: the idea that humans consist of two fundamentally different substances, an immortal, immaterial soul temporarily housed in a mortal, material body. This framework, rooted in Plato and developed through centuries of Hellenistic philosophy, has profoundly shaped how Western readers understand biblical language about human nature.

**The problem is that this framework is foreign to the biblical authors — and postdates them.**

Even on the latest critical dating schemes, the Torah reached its final form no later than the 5th century BC, contemporary with or before Plato (c. 428–348 BC). Traditional dating places the composition of Genesis and the Pentateuch centuries earlier still. The ANE literature that forms the conceptual world of the biblical authors — Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Ugaritic — predates Plato by a millennium or more. Greek philosophical dualism is therefore not the interpretive background of the biblical authors; it is a later framework imposed backward onto texts written in a completely different conceptual world. The burden of proof falls on those who read Platonic categories into Hebrew Scripture, not on those who read the Hebrew on its own terms.

When modern readers encounter words like "soul" and "spirit" in English Bibles, they instinctively import Platonic categories: an immortal ghost-part that exists independently of the body and survives death automatically. But the Hebrew terms behind these translations (*nephesh* and *ruach*) do not carry these meanings. Understanding what these words actually meant to the biblical authors is essential for reading Scripture on its own terms.

**A note on what follows:** Clarifying that *nephesh* does not mean "immortal soul" is **not** the same as denying conscious existence after death. Scripture does point to an intermediate state and to hope beyond the grave. But those teachings must be derived from texts that actually address them (such as Jesus' words to the thief on the cross, Paul's "to depart and be with Christ," or the parable of the rich man and Lazarus), **not** from the bare definition of Hebrew anthropological vocabulary. The error we are correcting is the assumption that "soul" *by definition* means an immortal, separable, consciously surviving entity. That assumption comes from Plato, not from Moses.

### What Greek Dualism Teaches

In Platonic thought, the soul (Greek: *psychē*) is the true self: eternal, rational, and divine in origin. The body (Greek: *sōma*) is a prison or tomb for the soul, something to be escaped. Death is liberation: the immortal soul finally freed from its material cage. This view assumes a fundamental opposition between spirit and matter, with spirit being good and matter being inferior or evil.

This framework entered Christian theology through the early church's engagement with Greek philosophy, and it has colored Bible translation ever since. The historical trajectory is traceable: the LXX (3rd–2nd c. BC) translated *nephesh* as Greek *psychē*, beginning the terminological overlap. Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BC–50 AD) systematically fused Hebrew Scripture with Platonic philosophy. By the second century, Apologists such as Justin Martyr were adopting soul-immortality language in dialogue with Greek culture. Origen (3rd c. AD) applied the Platonic framework extensively — including body-as-prison imagery and the pre-existence of souls. It was Augustine of Hippo (354–430 AD) who synthesized Neoplatonism with Christian theology comprehensively enough that his anthropology became the default framework for the Western church through the medieval period and the Reformation. The Greek dualist reading of *nephesh* and *ruach* is therefore not the position of the biblical authors, the ANE world, or the earliest Christian interpreters; it is the position of a church increasingly shaped by its philosophical environment, systematized through one theologian's enormous influence.

### Nephesh: What the Hebrew Bible Actually Says About the "Soul"

The key Hebrew term is נֶפֶשׁ (*nephesh*), which appears over 750 times in the Old Testament. It does **not** mean "immortal soul." Its semantic range includes:

- **Throat/neck** (the physical organ): "water has come up to my *nephesh*" (Jonah 2:5)  
- **Breath/life**: "the *nephesh* of all flesh is its blood" (Lev 17:14)  
- **Appetite/desire**: "his *nephesh* craves" (Deut 12:20)  
- **Living being/creature**: animals are *nephesh* (Gen 1:20, 24; 2:19)  
- **Person/self**: "my *nephesh*" often simply means "I" or "me"  
- **A corpse**: a dead *nephesh* (Num 6:6)

The last two points are crucial. If *nephesh* meant "immortal soul," the phrase "dead nephesh" would be nonsense. And if only humans had immortal souls, why does Genesis use the same word for fish, birds, and livestock?

**Most Christians do not realize that animals have a nephesh.** Genesis 1:20, 24 and 2:19 all apply *nephesh* to animals, the very same word used for humans in Genesis 2:7. This single fact should immediately disrupt how we process the English word "soul." Whatever nephesh means, it cannot mean "the immortal part that distinguishes humans from animals," because Scripture applies it to animals without qualification.

The foundational text is Genesis 2:7: "The LORD God formed man from the dust of the ground and breathed the breath of life into his nostrils, and the man became a living *nephesh*." Notice: Adam does not *receive* a nephesh; he *becomes* one. The human being **is** a nephesh: an integrated, embodied, living self. There is no separable soul inserted into a body.

### Ruach: What the Hebrew Bible Actually Says About the "Spirit"

The Hebrew term רוּחַ (*ruach*) appears over 375 times in the Old Testament. Like nephesh, it has a wide semantic range:

- **Wind**: "God made a *ruach* to pass over the earth" (Gen 8:1)  
- **Breath**: "all in whose nostrils was the breath \[neshamah\] of the *ruach* of life" (Gen 7:22)  
- **Life-force**: "the *ruach* returns to God who gave it" (Eccl 12:7)  
- **Disposition/attitude**: "a broken *ruach*" (Psa 51:17); "a hasty *ruach*" (Prov 14:29)  
- **The Spirit of God**: "the *ruach* of God was hovering over the waters" (Gen 1:2)

The key insight is that ruach, when applied to humans, refers to the **animating life-breath that comes from God and returns to God at death.** It is not a separable "spirit-part" that constitutes "the real you" and floats off to conscious existence when the body dies. Ecclesiastes 12:7 ("the breath returns to God who gave it") is often read as teaching that an immortal spirit departs to heaven at death. But the verse is teaching **dependence on God for life itself**, not conscious afterlife. When a person dies, they stop breathing. The breath that God gave returns to its source. This is poetic language for mortality, not a technical statement about the intermediate state.

**Most Christians do not realize that animals have ruach.** Ecclesiastes 3:19-21 states explicitly: "For what happens to the children of man and what happens to the beasts is the same; as one dies, so dies the other. **They all have the same ruach.** Man has no advantage over the beasts, for all is vanity. All go to one place. All are from the dust, and to dust all return. Who knows whether the ruach of man goes upward and the ruach of the beast goes down into the earth?" If ruach meant "immortal spirit that consciously survives death," we would have to say animals have immortal spirits too. Qoheleth actually *questions* whether there is any functional difference between human and animal ruach. This text is devastating to the Platonic framework.

Note that ruach and nephesh overlap significantly and are **not** divisible parts of a human being. Both can refer to the whole person. Both can describe emotions and dispositions. The Hebrew conception is of a unified self, not a composite of separable components. Job 34:14-15 demonstrates this clearly: "If he should set his heart to it and gather to himself his *spirit* \[ruach\] and his *breath* \[neshamah\], all flesh would perish together, and man would return to dust." The parallelism shows ruach and neshamah functioning as near-synonyms for the animating life-breath, not as distinct metaphysical parts. Attempts to diagram "which part does what" miss the point entirely.

### The "Departing Soul" Passages

Several passages describe nephesh or ruach "departing" at death. These are often cited as proof-texts for the soul leaving the body. But in context, they describe life ending, not a ghost exiting.

**Genesis 35:18** (Rachel's death): "As her life \[nephesh\] was departing (for she was dying), she called his name Ben-oni." Traditional: "as her soul was departing." But nephesh here means life. Her life was leaving her. This is not a description of a ghost floating away; it is a description of dying.

**1 Kings 17:21-22** (Elijah raises the widow's son): Elijah prays, "let this child's life \[nephesh\] return to him," and "the child's life \[nephesh\] returned to him, and he lived." Traditional: "let this child's soul return." This is resuscitation. Life returned; the child revived. Not a disembodied soul re-entering a corpse.

**Psalm 31:5** (quoted by Jesus on the cross, Luke 23:46): "Into your hand I commit my life \[ruach\]." Traditional: "I commit my spirit." This is an act of trust, entrusting one's life to God's care. Jesus used these words at his death. This is not a statement about an immortal spirit departing to conscious existence; it is an expression of trust in the face of death.

**Psalm 146:4**: "When his breath \[ruach\] departs, he returns to the earth; on that very day his thoughts perish." This is critical: when the breath leaves, **thoughts cease**. The person's conscious planning and thinking end at death. This directly contradicts the Platonic framework of conscious survival.

**Isaiah 53:10-12** (the Suffering Servant): "when his life \[nephesh\] is made an offering for guilt... the anguish of his life \[nephesh\]... he poured out his life \[nephesh\] to death." Traditional: "his soul." But the Servant is not offering a separable soul-part; he is giving his whole self, his life, unto death. The translation "life" captures the totality of the Servant's self-giving.

### Why This Matters

The stakes are not merely academic. Greek dualism has shaped how Christians think about:

- **Death and resurrection**: If the soul is naturally immortal and escapes the body at death, bodily resurrection becomes optional or even undesirable. But the biblical hope is resurrection: the restoration of embodied life in a renewed creation. Paul calls the resurrection the hinge of the entire faith (1 Cor 15:14-19).  
    
- **The goodness of creation**: If matter is inferior to spirit, the physical world is devalued. But Genesis declares creation "very good," and the incarnation affirms that God himself took on flesh.  
    
- **Ethics and the body**: Dualism can lead either to asceticism (punishing the evil body) or licentiousness (what happens to the body doesn't matter spiritually). Biblical ethics treats embodied life as integral to who we are.  
    
- **The nature of salvation**: Greek thought offers escape from the body. Biblical thought offers resurrection of the body: transformation, not evacuation.

### How This Translation Handles Nephesh, Ruach, and Psychē

We face a practical challenge: "soul" and "spirit" appear in virtually every English Bible, and readers are deeply familiar with them. Changing every occurrence would make the text feel foreign in ways that do not serve readers.

Our approach is **targeted correction**:

1. **Where "soul" actively misleads, we change the text.** Ezekiel 18:4 ("The soul who sins shall die") is constantly misread as teaching about the eternal fate of immortal souls. But *nephesh* here simply means "the person." We render it: "The one who sins is the one who will die."  
     
2. **Where "departing soul" language imports dualism, we use "life."** BSB v3 has already addressed Genesis 35:18 and 1 Kings 17:21-22 in ways that remove the soul language. The principle still applies at Luke 8:55 and other passages not yet corrected in BSB.  
     
3. **Where ruach describes the breath returning to God, we use "breath."** Ecclesiastes 12:7 becomes "the breath returns to God who gave it"; Psalm 146:4 becomes "when his breath departs... his thoughts perish." This preserves the connection to Genesis 2:7 without importing conscious-survival assumptions.  
     
4. **Where "spirit" fits an act of trust, we use "life" with footnote.** Psalm 31:5 / Luke 23:46 becomes "Into your hand I commit my life" with a footnote explaining: "Heb. ruach. An act of trust, entrusting one's life to God's care."  
     
5. **Where parallel passages create internal inconsistency, we harmonize.** Mark 8:35 says "whoever loses his *psychē* for my sake will save it." Most translations render this "life." But Mark 8:36-37 and Matthew 16:26 use the same Greek word and render it "soul." We use "life" consistently in these passages.  
     
6. **Where "soul" is poetic and does not mislead, we keep it with footnotes.** "Bless the LORD, O my soul" (Psalm 103:1) functions as self-address; the psalmist is calling himself to worship. We retain "soul" here but add explanatory footnotes at key locations.  
     
7. **At critical junctures, we add footnotes.** Deuteronomy 6:5 (the Shema), Matthew 10:28 (the body/soul contrast), and 1 Thessalonians 5:23 ("spirit, soul, and body") all receive footnotes explaining that the biblical vocabulary does not map onto Greek dualism.  
     
8. **Where parallelism proves our point, we preserve it.** Job 34:14-15 ("his spirit and his breath") keeps both terms, showing they function as synonyms for the animating life-force, not as distinct metaphysical components.

## Hermeneutical Foundation

Two pillars drive every translation decision.

### Pillar 1: Biblical Theology

*What does the Bible say about the Bible?*

Scripture interprets Scripture. The biblical authors wrote with awareness of prior revelation, building on theological frameworks established in earlier texts. A faithful translation preserves the connections the authors themselves made: the way the New Testament reads the Old, the way prophets draw on Torah, and the way Revelation draws on Daniel, Ezekiel, and Isaiah.

This means:

- Translation choices should preserve intertextual connections the authors intended  
- Later theological categories (Platonic, Augustinian, medieval) should not be imported where the biblical authors did not have them  
- The Bible's own theological vocabulary should be rendered transparently, not replaced with post-biblical substitutes  
- When the NT interprets an OT passage under inspiration, that interpretation has authority and may inform translation

### Pillar 2: Ancient Context

*What did the authors mean when they wrote?*

The biblical authors wrote within real cultural and conceptual worlds. The Old Testament is ancient Near Eastern. The New Testament is Second Temple Jewish within a Greco-Roman setting. Understanding what they meant requires taking that world seriously.

This includes:

- The divine council and its members (elohim, sons of God, holy ones, host of heaven)  
- The multi-tiered cosmos (heavens, earth, underworld; spiritual beings governing nations)  
- The reality of spiritual beings (demons, territorial powers, the Accuser)  
- Hebrew and Second Temple Jewish anthropology rather than imported philosophical dualisms  
- Sheol as the realm of the dead, distinct from later heaven/hell simplifications

These two pillars, standing on the bedrock of sola scriptura, drive every correction in this translation.

### The Logic Test

When choosing between English words, we ask: *If an ancient Israelite (or first-century Jew) who understood English were shown the available options, which word would they say best represents what they meant?* This keeps us accountable to the text rather than to modern comfort. We are not asking what makes contemporary readers feel at ease; we are asking what the biblical authors actually said. The firmament discussion below (Part 1\) illustrates this test in detail.

## Part 1: Cosmic Structure and Space

The biblical authors inhabited a world with a specific shape. The following terms preserve that cosmology without requiring readers to adopt any particular scientific model.

### Firmament (רָקִיעַ / raqia)

The Hebrew noun *raqia* derives from the verb *raqa*, meaning "to beat out" or "to spread by hammering," like hammering gold into thin sheets (cf. Exodus 39:3). When God creates the *raqia* on Day Two, the word naturally carries the sense of something beaten-out and firm. This same word appears in Ezekiel 1:22-26, where the prophet sees "the likeness of a firmament... like the gleaming of ice," a solid surface upon which Yahweh's throne rests. Job 37:18 asks, "Can you, like him, spread out the skies, hard as a cast metal mirror?"

Many modern translations render *raqia* as "expanse," a word suggesting openness and empty space, nearly the opposite of what the Hebrew denotes. The Hebrew denotes hammering, hardness, and a firm barrier. "Expanse" does not preserve the fullness of this lexeme; "firmament" does.

**A note on "firmament":** We are not entirely happy with this word. It sounds archaic, and for many readers it carries negative cosmological connotations (flat earth, pre-scientific ignorance, etc.). We explored alternatives extensively, even drifting into sci-fi terminology to see if something like "force field," "containment shell," or "forged canopy" might communicate the Hebrew sense of a hard, hammered-out barrier. Nothing worked. Every alternative either imported modern concepts foreign to the text or failed to map onto a specific Hebrew word. "Firmament" remains the most accurate English term for *raqia* precisely because it has no other use in modern English. It forces readers to ask what it means rather than assuming they already know.

**The logic test:** Our guiding question for translation decisions is this: *If an ancient Israelite who understood English were shown the available options, which word would they say best represents what they meant?* For *raqia*, the answer is clearly "firmament," not "expanse." The ancient reader would recognize "firmament" as capturing what they understood about the sky: something solid, hammered-out, a barrier holding back waters above. They would reject "expanse" as a mistranslation that erases the very thing they were describing. This test keeps us accountable to the text rather than to modern comfort. We are not requiring readers to adopt any particular scientific model; we are requiring the translation to say what the Hebrew says.

We rendered all occurrences of *raqia* as "firmament" (not "expanse," "vault," "dome," or "sky"):

### The Deep (תְּהוֹם / Tehom)

The Hebrew תְּהוֹם (*tehom*) refers to the primordial waters, the cosmic deep that existed before creation and still churns beneath the ordered world. It appears in Genesis 1:2 ("darkness was over the face of the deep"), in the flood narrative where "the fountains of the great deep" (תְּהוֹם רַבָּה, *tehom rabbah*) burst forth (Gen 7:11; 8:2), and throughout poetic texts describing Yahweh's mastery over chaotic waters (Psa 33:7; 104:6; Prov 8:27-28).

We rendered תְּהוֹם consistently as "the deep" to preserve the cosmological vocabulary. This is not merely "ocean" or "sea" but the primordial abyss that Yahweh subdued at creation and restrains by decree.

### Host of Heaven (צְבָא הַשָּׁמַיִם / tseva hashamayim)

The phrase "host of heaven" appears in contexts of worship (Deut 4:19; 17:3; 2 Kgs 21:3), divine council assembly (1 Kgs 22:19), and cosmic imagery (Isa 34:4; 45:12).

In ancient cosmology, astral bodies and spiritual beings are not separate categories. The stars are not merely physical objects; they are associated with divine beings who govern and inhabit them. When Israel is forbidden to worship the host of heaven, the prohibition targets spiritual powers, not astronomy.

We preserved "host of heaven" with the understanding that this phrase denotes spiritual beings in their cosmic role. We did not demythologize to merely physical stars when the context treats them as animate agents or objects of worship.

| Isa 34:4 | Cosmic judgment | Host of heaven dissolves; armies fall |
| Jer 19:13 | Rooftop worship | Incense burned to the host of heaven |

### Stairway (סֻלָּם / sullam)

In Genesis 28:12, Jacob dreams of a סֻלָּם (*sullam*) connecting earth and heaven. The word is a hapax legomenon (occurring only here in the Hebrew Bible). The related verb *salal* means "to heap up" or "to cast up" (as in building a ramp or highway). The term does not denote a portable ladder but a fixed stairway or ramp.

The imagery evokes the Mesopotamian ziggurat: a stepped temple structure with external stairs ascending to a shrine at the top where the deity dwelt. Jacob's response confirms this: he calls the place "the house of God" (בֵּית אֵל, *beth-el*) and "the gate of heaven" (v. 17). A freestanding ladder does not evoke "house"; a temple with a stairway does. The parallels to the Tower of Babel narrative (Gen 11:4: "a tower with its top in the heavens") reinforce the ziggurat background.

The theological point is not that Jacob saw a literal Babylonian structure, but that what pagan ziggurats aspired to represent (the meeting place of heaven and earth, where gods descend and humans may approach), this vision reveals as a divine reality. YHWH stands at the top, his messengers traverse the stairs, and Jacob stands at the threshold of God's house.

"Ladder" obscures this temple theology. "Stairway" preserves it.

| Reference | BSB | AAB |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Gen 28:12 | ladder | stairway |

**Footnote in translation (Gen 28:12):** "Heb. *sullam*, a hapax; related to *salal*, 'to heap up, cast up (a ramp).' The imagery evokes the stairway of a temple or ziggurat, which Jacob identifies as 'the house of God' (v. 17)."

## Part 2: Divine Beings and Council

The following section addresses how we rendered terms for spiritual beings other than Yahweh.

### Elohim and Divine Council Language

When *elohim* refers to the God of Israel, we rendered it as "God."

When *elohim* refers to other spiritual beings (plural predication, or context indicates beings other than Yahweh), we rendered it as "gods," "divine beings," "spirits," or "heavenly beings" as context fits.

We did not reduce supernatural *elohim* to "judges," "rulers," or "mighty ones."

| Reference | Rendering |
| :---- | :---- |
| Gen 3:5 | "you will be like gods" |
| Gen 3:22 | "like one of us" |
| Psalm 82:1 | "God stands in the divine council; among the gods he renders judgment" |
| Psalm 82:6 | "You are gods, sons of the Most High, all of you" |
| Exod 21:6 | "bring him to God" (singular) |
| 1 Sam 28:13 | "a spirit coming up from the earth" |
| Gen 6:2, 4 | "sons of God" (never "sons of nobles" or "descendants of Seth") |

| Psalm 29:1; 89:6 | "sons of God" or "heavenly beings" |

**A note on Genesis 3:5 and 3:22:** The Hebrew כֵּאלֹהִים (*ke'elohim*) in Genesis 3:5 means "like elohim/divine beings" — the same word used throughout for members of the assembly of heavenly beings. Most modern translations render it "like God" (singular), following the Trinitarian reading. The AAB renders "like gods" because the plural is what the Hebrew says and because Genesis 3:22 confirms it: God himself says the man "has become like one of us." The "us" refers to the assembly of heavenly beings present with God, who were present at creation and shouted for joy when God laid the foundations of the earth (Job 38:7). The Trinitarian reading remains a legitimate one; the text does not exclude it. But the Hebrew plural should not be flattened to a singular when the context supports it.

**A note on Genesis 6 and the history of interpretation:** The reading of "sons of God" in Genesis 6 as supernatural beings is unbroken from the earliest recorded interpretation through approximately the second century AD; no alternative was entertained until Julius Africanus (c. 221 AD), who proposed the Sethite reading (sons of God as righteous descendants of Seth) but did not himself commit to it. Second Temple sources are unambiguous: the Book of Watchers (1 Enoch 1–36, 3rd–2nd century BC) builds an entire narrative on this reading; Jubilees (2nd century BC), the Book of Giants (found among the Dead Sea Scrolls), Philo (*De Gigantibus*), and Josephus (*Antiquities* 1.3.1) all identify the sons of God as angelic beings. The early church fathers inherited this reading without controversy: Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Tertullian, and Origen all read Genesis 6 as describing divine beings taking human wives. The broader ANE supplies similar traditions of divine beings interbreeding with humans; the Mesopotamian apkallu tradition is the closest conceptual parallel. These accounts are what one would expect if the sons of God reading is correct: if the event occurred, the memory of it would surface across cultures that inherited the same ancient world, distorted by their own religious frameworks but preserving the basic shape of the story. Augustine popularized the Sethite reading in *City of God* (c. 413–426 AD), after which it became the dominant position in the Western church and has remained so through most of Christian history. That consensus is now shifting. The discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls, along with broader advances in the study of ancient Jewish literature and the world behind the Old Testament, has brought the original supernatural reading back into serious discussion, and it is increasingly recognized as the correct reading, given that it is what the biblical authors themselves held.

### Holy Ones (קְדֹשִׁים / ἅγιοι)

Traditional translations often render קְדֹשִׁים (*qedoshim*) and ἅγιοι (*hagioi*) as "saints." This term carries post-biblical connotations: canonized individuals in Catholic and Orthodox usage, or a general term for believers in Protestant usage. Neither captures the full range of the Hebrew and Greek.

The words simply mean "set-apart ones" or "holy ones." In divine council contexts (Psa 89:5, 7; Deut 33:2-3; Zech 14:5), these are heavenly beings. In other contexts, they refer to Israel as God's set-apart people or to NT believers.

We rendered קְדֹשִׁים and ἅγιοι as "holy ones" throughout (89 occurrences), preserving the term's flexibility and avoiding post-biblical baggage.

### The Accuser

In Job 1-2 and Zechariah 3, Hebrew has the definite article: הַשָּׂטָן (*hasatan*, "the satan"). Grammatically, this is a title ("the accuser/adversary"), not a proper name. A translation should reflect that grammar.

| Reference | Rendering |
| :---- | :---- |
| Job 1:6-12; 2:1-7 | "the Accuser" |
| Zech 3:1-2 | "the Accuser" |
| 1 Chron 21:1 | "Satan" (capitalized) |

**Note on 1 Chronicles 21:1:** The Hebrew lacks the definite article, indicating the term functions as a proper name rather than a title. We rendered this as "Satan" (capitalized). A footnote in the translation reads: "Heb. *satan*, without the definite article, indicating a proper name rather than a title. Rendered 'Satan' here, as distinct from Job 1-2 and Zechariah 3 where the article is present."

**Note on the Job and Zechariah footnote:** The BSB includes a footnote at Job 1:6 reading "that is, the Accuser or the Adversary; here and throughout Job." The AAB replaces this with a footnote that explains the grammatical basis: "Heb. *hasatan*, 'accuser' or 'adversary' (lexical range). The definite article indicates a title, not a proper name. Rendered 'the Accuser' here and throughout Job and Zechariah 3, where the article is consistently present."

**Note on the New Testament:** Greek proper names routinely take the definite article — Jesus, Paul, Peter — without that article signaling a title. The presence of the article with Σατανᾶς in the NT does not carry the same grammatical signal that הַשָּׂטָן carries in Hebrew, where the article marks a title rather than a proper name. The AAB therefore renders Σατανᾶς as "Satan" throughout the NT except in four passages where the NT author is most clearly invoking the specific OT scenes in Job 1–2 and Zechariah 3. The basis for these four exceptions is canonical and intertextual, not grammatical.

## Part 3: Underworld and Death

The biblical texts distinguish between multiple locations and states associated with death and the afterlife. The following section explains how we preserved these distinctions.

### Underworld Terms

We maintained distinctions between the various underworld and afterlife terms:

| Term | Our Rendering | Notes |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| שְׁאוֹל (Sheol) | "Sheol" (default); "grave," "pit," "the depths" where appropriate | Never "hell." |
| ᾅδης (Hades) | "Hades" (always) | BSB v3 already correct; never "hell." |
| γέεννα (Gehenna) | "hell" (always) | Always footnote "Gk. *gehenna*." |
| ταρταρόω (Tartarus) | "Tartarus" | Prison for rebellious divine beings; never "hell." |
| ἄβυσσος (Abyss) | "the Abyss" | BSB v3 mostly correct; Rev 9:1–2 still need "pit of" removed. |
| נָחַם in Sheol (Ezek 31:16; 32:31) | "filled with regret" | Never "comforted." |

**Note on Gehenna:** Gehenna (γέεννα) is the only underworld term where "hell" is appropriate. Unlike Sheol/Hades (realm of the dead, not punishment) or Tartarus/Abyss (prisons for spiritual beings), Gehenna refers to the place of final fiery judgment: the Valley of Hinnom, associated with child sacrifice and divine wrath. This is what "hell" means in modern English, so the semantic mapping is accurate.

**Our policy:** We render all occurrences of γέεννα as "hell" with the footnote "Gk. *gehenna*." This communicates the meaning accurately to English readers while preserving source-language transparency for those who want it.

**Abyss note:** The Abyss (ἄβυσσος) appears in Revelation 9:1–2, 11; 11:7; 17:8; 20:1–3. BSB v3 has adopted "Abyss" throughout but retains the redundant "pit of" at 9:1–2. It functions as a prison for hostile spiritual beings and connects to the Tartarus concept in 2 Peter 2:4.

### Rephaim (רְפָאִים / repha'im)

The Hebrew word רְפָאִים (*repha'im*) appears in two distinct contexts: (1) as an ancient race of giants in the Transjordan region (Gen 14:5; 15:20; Deut 2:11, 20; 3:11), and (2) as the shades or spirits of the dead dwelling in Sheol (Job 26:5; Psa 88:10; Prov 2:18; 9:18; 21:16; Isa 14:9; 26:14, 19).

These two uses are connected. In the biblical worldview, the mighty warrior-kings of old, the Rephaim, descended to Sheol at death and became its inhabitants. This echoes Ugaritic texts where *rpum* refers to deified royal ancestors summoned in funerary rituals. Isaiah 14:9 makes this connection explicit: "Sheol beneath is stirred up to meet you... it rouses the Rephaim to greet you, all who were leaders of the earth."

Modern translations typically render *repha'im* in underworld contexts as "the dead," "the departed," or "the shades." These generic terms obscure the specific theological vocabulary and sever the connection to the giant lineage. More importantly, they flatten Isaiah 26:14, 19, which is a resurrection polemic. The prophet declares that the pagan Rephaim "will not rise" (v. 14), but Yahweh's dead "shall live; their bodies shall rise" (v. 19). The contrast depends on the reader recognizing *repha'im* as a specific category of underworld inhabitants, not a generic term for "dead people."

Preserving "Rephaim" in all eight poetic occurrences maintains the biblical vocabulary, preserves intertextual connections, and allows the resurrection polemic of Isaiah 26 to land with full force.

### Abaddon (אֲבַדּוֹן / 'abaddon)

The Hebrew word אֲבַדּוֹן (*'abaddon*) means "destruction" or "place of destruction" and appears six times in the OT as a poetic parallel to Sheol. Most translations render it generically as "destruction," obscuring the specific underworld vocabulary. Revelation 9:11 preserves both Hebrew "Abaddon" and Greek "Apollyon" as the name of the angel of the Abyss.

Preserving "Abaddon" maintains the specific underworld vocabulary and connects OT usage to Revelation 9:11. For consistency with Sheol, Hades, Gehenna, Tartarus, and Abyss preservation, Abaddon should also be preserved.

### Death Personified (מָוֶת / Thanatos)

In numerous passages, Death is not merely an event but a power, an enemy, even a being with agency. Job, Psalms, Hosea, and Revelation all personify Death in ways that align with ancient Near Eastern concepts of Mot (the death deity) and the NT portrayal of Death as the "last enemy" (1 Cor 15:26).

We capitalized "Death" when the context treats it as a personified power rather than a mere event:

| Reference | Context |
| :---- | :---- |
| Job 18:13 | "the firstborn of Death" |
| Job 28:22 | "Abaddon and Death say..." |
| Psalm 49:14 | "Death shall be their shepherd" |
| Psalm 89:48 | "deliver his soul from the power of Death" |
| Hosea 13:14 | "O Death, where are your plagues?" |
| Habakkuk 2:5 | "his greed is as wide as Death" |

This capitalization visually reinforces that Death is a defeated power, not merely a natural process, and connects OT personification to Paul's resurrection theology and Revelation's final vision.

## Part 4: Spiritual Beings and Idolatry

The following section addresses terms for demonic beings and the entities associated with Israel's idolatry.

### Demons (שֵׁדִים / shedim)

Where Hebrew שֵׁדִים (*shedim*) appears (Deut 32:17; Psa 106:37), we rendered "demons," not "false gods" or "idols." The text affirms these are real spiritual entities to whom Israel sacrificed.

### Keening Creatures / Ohim (אֹחִים / ohim)

Heb. אֹחִים (*ohim*), 'keening ones' or 'keening creatures.' Appears only in Isaiah 13:21. The root points to mournful howling. In Hebrew the -*im* ending marks plural beings rather than abstract qualities; the context places *ohim* alongside a sequence of creatures and spirits none of which is an abstraction; and the ANE material for this desolation formula consistently populates ruins with hostile supernatural beings. The LXX renders it as ἦχος (*echos*, "sound/resonance") rather than a named creature — that reading is the earliest on record, but the -*im* ending, the surrounding company of beings, and Revelation 18:2 all point in the same direction. Revelation reads Babylon's desolation as "a dwelling place of demons, a haunt for every unclean spirit," drawing directly on Isaiah 13 and 34; the *ohim* fill those houses as part of that company. The Songs of the Sage (4Q510, late 1st c. BCE) lists "howlers" alongside demons, Lilith, and spirits of the bastards in a single exorcism formula, confirming that Second Temple readers identified this creature class from the desolation texts as a recognized demonic category.

### Sirens / Daughters of the Ostrich (בְּנוֹת יַעֲנָה / benot ya'anah)

Heb. בְּנוֹת יַעֲנָה (*benot ya'anah*). Most dictionaries and English translations identify it as "daughters of the ostrich" or simply "ostriches." The LXX translators nonetheless rendered it as σειρῆνες (*seirēnes*): supernatural beings with a woman's head and a bird's body whose wailing voices lured the living to their deaths. The bird-form, the lamenting context (Micah 1:8 uses the term as a figure of mournful wailing; Job 30:29 pairs it with תַּנִּים / *tannim* as a figure of desolation), and the surrounding company of supernatural beings all point in the same direction. Revelation 18:2 reads Babylon's desolation as "a dwelling place of demons, a haunt for every unclean spirit," drawing directly on Isaiah 13 and 34. Whatever *benot ya'anah* names as a creature, Revelation requires that these ruined cities be populated by hostile spiritual beings. "Sirens" is the right English choice precisely because modern English speakers inherit a Greco-Roman cultural frame: the word already carries what the LXX translators were reaching for — bird-woman form, wailing voice, a deadly supernatural presence.

Appears at Isaiah 13:21, Isaiah 34:13, and Jeremiah 50:39.

### Goat-Demons (שְׂעִירִים / se'irim)

Heb. שְׂעִירִים (*se'irim*), 'hairy ones' or 'goat-beings'; singular שָׂעִיר (*sa'ir*). Leviticus 17:7 prohibits Israel from sacrificing to the *se'irim*, which means they were receiving sacrifices. 2 Chronicles 11:15 records Jeroboam appointing priests for them alongside the golden calves. The LXX renders *se'irim* as δαιμόνια (*daimonia*) in both Isaiah 13:21 and 34:14; Revelation 18:2 uses the same word. "Satyr" is not used as the translation because it imports Greek mythology as the primary frame. "Goat demon" names what the canon establishes: a hostile supernatural being associated with the goat complex, receiving worship in YHWH's place.

BSB v3 has already adopted "goat demons" at Leviticus 17:7 and 2 Chronicles 11:15. The AAB change applies to Isaiah 13:21 and Isaiah 34:14.

### Howlers / Iyyim (אִיִּים / iyyim)

Heb. אִיִּים (*iyyim*), 'howlers.' Appear only in this desolation formula (Isa 13:22; 34:14; Jer 50:39), always alongside צִיִּים (*tsiyyim*, 'desert dwellers'). The root points to mournful howling or wailing. The LXX renders the word as ὀνοκένταυρος (*onokentauros*, "donkey-centaur") — not a wild animal but a being of the human-animal hybrid class: part human, part beast, belonging to neither category fully, and therefore uncanny and dangerous. This is the same broad register as the centaur and the satyr in Greek thought, or the composite guardian figures of Mesopotamian iconography — beings that transgress the boundary between the human and animal worlds. That the Greek translators reached for this category rather than any natural creature shows they read *iyyim* as a supernatural being, not a creature of the field. Mizrahi and Patmore (2019) have established that the demon list in the Songs of the Sage (4Q510, late 1st c. BCE) derives its *iyyim* directly from the Isaiah desolation passages through a documented exegetical tradition, confirming that Second Temple readers recognized this creature class as a demonic category. That reading is consistent with the canonical placement of *iyyim* in immediate parallel with *se'irim* (goat demons who received cultic worship, Lev 17:7) and with Lilith in the same verse. Revelation 18:2 reads Babylon's desolation as "a dwelling place of demons, a haunt for every unclean spirit," drawing directly on Isaiah 13 and 34; the *iyyim* fill that company. "Howlers" names what the Hebrew root attests; the LXX's rendering suggests the ancient translators understood them as belonging to the category of composite, boundary-crossing beings.

### Dragons / Tannim (תַּנִּים / תַּנִּין / tannim / tannin)

Heb. תַּנִּים / תַּנִּין (*tannim* / *tannin*), 'sea-dragon' or 'chaos-creature.' Canonical range runs from chaos-dragon (Ps 74:13; Isa 27:1; Isa 51:9, where *tannin* parallels Rahab) to the devouring sea-beast of creation (Gen 1:21; Job 7:12). Within the same Babylon oracle corpus, Jeremiah 51:34 applies the word directly to Nebuchadnezzar: "he has swallowed me like a dragon." The LXX renders δράκοντες (*drakontes*) throughout; the KJV renders "dragons." Most modern translations default to "jackals," a choice driven by naturalistic assumptions about the vocabulary of desolation rather than by the canonical weight of the term; throughout the Hebrew Bible *tannin* is overwhelmingly a chaos adversary, not a wild dog.

Appears at Isaiah 13:22, Isaiah 34:13, and multiple other desolation texts.

### Lilith (לִילִית / lilit)

Heb. לִילִית (*lilit*), 'night-demon.' BDB identifies her as "a female night-demon haunting desolate Edom, borrowed from Babylonian tradition." The Babylonian *lilitu* and *ardat lili* are supernatural beings of desolate places and night. The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran renders the word in the plural (*liliyyot*), suggesting a class rather than a unique figure. The Songs of the Sage (4Q510, late 1st c. BCE) contains the earliest unambiguous identification of Lilith as a supernatural being within a catalog of demons; the passage lists her alongside "spirits of the bastards, demons, howlers, and desert dwellers" — the precise cluster of beings populating the Isaiah and Jeremiah desolation texts. That text is secondary to the canonical witness, but it confirms that Second Temple readers recognized these figures, Lilith included, as hostile spiritual beings rather than unusual wildlife.

In Isaiah 34:14, we preserved "Lilith" rather than obscuring the term as "screech owl" or "night creature."

### Azazel (עֲזָאזֵל / 'aza'zel)

In Leviticus 16, we preserved "Azazel" as a proper name (the recipient of the goat sent into the wilderness) rather than translating it as "scapegoat." The scapegoat is the goat; Azazel is where (or to whom) it goes.

## Part 6: Sacrificial Terminology

The Levitical sacrificial system uses technical vocabulary that traditional translations have rendered in ways that import post-biblical soteriology. The following corrections restore the Hebrew semantic range.

### Purification Offering (*ḥaṭṭāʾt* / חַטָּאת, "sin offering")

Traditional translations render חַטָּאת as "sin offering," but this miscommunicates the sacrifice's function to English readers in ways the Hebrew does not permit.

**The blood goes to the sanctuary, not the sinner.** Throughout Leviticus 4 and 16, the blood of the חַטָּאת is applied to the altar, the tent of meeting, and the mercy seat (*kapporeth* / כַּפֹּרֶת, "atonement cover"), not to the offerer (Lev 4:25; 16:14–16). If the primary function were moral expiation of the individual, this is exactly backwards. The ritual decontaminates sacred space so God's presence can remain among his people.

**The offering is required where no moral failure occurred.** The חַטָּאת is prescribed after childbirth (Lev 12), after contact with a corpse (Num 19), and after recovery from skin disease (Lev 14). A woman who has just given birth is not being charged with sin. Yet she requires the same sacrificial mechanism as one who sinned inadvertently (*shegagah* / שְׁגָגָה, "inadvertency"). The ritual addresses a condition rather than exclusively a moral offense. Impurity (*ṭumʾāh* / טֻמְאָה, "uncleanness") has many causes; moral failure is one, ritual contamination another. The חַטָּאת addresses the condition regardless of cause.

**English "sin" cannot carry the Hebrew semantic range.** The Hebrew root *ḥāṭāʾ* (חטא, "to sin") carried a semantic range that included both moral failure and ritual contamination without moral component. An ancient Israelite held both uses simultaneously without confusion. English "sin" exclusively denotes moral failure. Translating חַטָּאת as "sin offering" does not render the Hebrew into English; it actively imports a category the Hebrew was not always invoking. A woman reading that she must bring a "sin offering" after childbirth either concludes that giving birth is morally defiling or that the text has gone wrong. The translation is the problem.

These three lines converge in the underlying logic of the system. Every חַטָּאת involves the death of an animal. Impurity of any kind, moral or ritual, creates a condition incompatible with the presence of a holy God, and a death-cleansing is required to address it. God then bears (*nasa* / נשׂא, "to carry, lift") the iniquity forward. Wrath is the consequence of that condition going unresolved, not the primary category the ritual addresses. The חַטָּאת restores fitness for life in God's household and sacred space.

**Milgrom's translation survives the challenge to his etymology.** Jacob Milgrom's Anchor Bible Commentary on Leviticus (1991–2001) argued that חַטָּאת derives from the Piel stem (*ḥiṭṭēʾ* / חטּא, "to purify, de-sin, decontaminate") rather than the Qal, and that "purification offering" follows from this derivation. Joseph Lam has since demonstrated that חַטָּאת follows a common Semitic *qaṭal(a)t* noun pattern and that the noun's dominant usage meaning "sin" renders the Piel derivation insufficient as a translation driver. Lam's critique stands. We do not rest our rendering on Milgrom's etymological case. We rest it on where the blood goes, who is required to bring the offering, and what English "sin" communicates to a reader who has never read Leviticus in Hebrew. Those arguments are independent of the etymology debate and sufficient without it.

We rendered all 114 occurrences of חַטָּאת as "purification offering."

### Restitution Offering (אָשָׁם / asham)

Traditional translations render אָשָׁם as "guilt offering," but the offering's function is reparation for damage done, not psychological guilt. The אָשָׁם is required when restitution is owed, whether to God or neighbor (Lev 5:14-6:7). The offerer must repay what was taken plus a penalty, and then bring the sacrifice.

We rendered all 34 occurrences of אָשָׁם as "restitution offering."

### Atonement Cover (כַּפֹּרֶת / *kapporet*)

Traditional translations render כַּפֹּרֶת as "mercy seat," but this imports a concept not found in the Hebrew. The כַּפֹּרֶת is the solid gold lid of the Ark of the Covenant, the place where Yahweh's presence dwelt between the cherubim (Exod 25:22). The blood of the purification offering is applied here on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:14–15).

"Mercy seat" comes from Tyndale via Luther's *Gnadenstuhl*, a theological interpretation rather than a translation. The Hebrew term derives from כִּפֶּר (*kipper*, "to cover, purge, make atonement") and describes the location of atonement ritual. "Atonement cover" renders the lexical meaning accurately. That this cover sits between the cherubim in the innermost chamber of God's house, the place from which he spoke and above which his glory appeared, carries its own theological force without requiring it to be stated in the translation itself.[^3]

We rendered all 23 occurrences of כַּפֹּרֶת as "atonement cover."

## Specific Passages

The passages in this section require individual treatment because they involve textual, interpretive, or theological questions that go beyond simple terminology.

### Morning Star, Son of Dawn (Isaiah 14:12)

The Hebrew phrase הֵילֵל בֶּן־שָׁחַר (*helel ben shachar*). *Helel* denotes brightness or the morning star (Venus at dawn). *Shachar* ("Dawn") appears in Ugaritic texts (KTU 1.23) as a son of El, the creator-God and chief deity of the Canaanite pantheon, and a member of the divine assembly. The Hebrew "sons of God" (בְּנֵי הָאֱלֹהִים, *bene ha-elohim*) belongs to the same category (cf. Job 1:6; Deut 32:8). The taunt of Isaiah 14 is a *mashal* (v. 4), a proverbial song, and *helel*'s aspiration ("I will ascend to heaven; above the stars of God I will set my throne on high," v. 13) maps onto the divine council cosmology: the fall from heaven is a degradation within the council, a stripping of station among the assembly of the divine sons. Psalm 82 operates on the same structure, where *elohim* are judged and sentenced to die "like men" for corrupting their governance role.

The KJV imported Latin tradition: "O Lucifer, son of the morning\!" But "Lucifer" is a Latin word that obscures the Hebrew phrase and its ancient Canaanite background.

The *helel* passage is not primarily speculation about the origin of Satan; it is a taunt song that draws on divine council imagery to pronounce the final verdict on the king of Babylon, whose pride replicated the primal pattern of a son of God grasping at the throne above his station.

**Our rendering:** "morning star, son of the dawn"

This translates what the Hebrew says while retaining the astronomical image (*helel* = the bright morning star) and preserving the divine council backdrop. Nothing here denies later biblical connections to spiritual rebellion; it simply refuses to replace Isaiah's Hebrew imagery with a Latin label that hides the ancient backdrop.

### Isaiah 7:14, Virgin

The Hebrew word עַלְמָה (*almah*) means "young woman of marriageable age." The word itself does not specify virginity (בְּתוּלָה, *betulah*, is more specific), though in the cultural context an unmarried *almah* would typically be presumed to be a virgin.

The LXX translators rendered *almah* as παρθένος (*parthenos*, "virgin"). Matthew 1:23, under inspiration, quotes this passage and applies it to Mary's virginal conception of Jesus. This is not later tradition interpreting Scripture; this is later Scripture interpreting earlier Scripture.

Because we hold both testaments as the inspired word of God, and because Scripture interprets Scripture, we rendered *almah* as "virgin" in Isaiah 7:14. The footnote clarifies: "Heb. *almah*, 'young woman of marriageable age.'"

This is categorically different from rendering "Lucifer" in Isaiah 14:12. "Lucifer" is Latin tradition replacing Hebrew. "Virgin" is NT Scripture clarifying OT Scripture. The former obscures the text; the latter illuminates it.

### The Cherub of Eden (Ezekiel 28:11-19)

The oracle against the king of Tyre in Ezekiel 28:11-19 employs imagery that exceeds any human referent: "You were in Eden, the garden of God... you were an anointed guardian cherub... you were blameless in your ways from the day you were created, until wickedness was found in you."

Many translations flatten this to the human king alone, treating Eden and cherub as rhetorical flourish. We preserved the supernatural layer without forcing a specific identification. The text presents a divine being who was granted access to God's holy mountain, adorned with glory, and cast down for pride.

Whether this describes the being later known as Satan, or another rebellious member of the divine council, or functions as mythic typology for human pride, the translation should not foreclose what the text itself suggests. We rendered the passage to preserve its supernatural imagery, letting readers and commentators draw connections as warranted.

### Deuteronomy 32:8, Sons of God

The end of verse 8 reads differently across the manuscript traditions. The MT reads "according to the number of the sons of Israel" (*bene yisrael*). The DSS (4QDeutj and related fragments) and the underlying Vorlage of the LXX read "according to the number of the sons of God" (*bene elohim*). The LXX itself renders this as "angels of God," which is a translation decision, not an independent textual variant; the DSS confirms that the source text behind the LXX read *bene elohim*.

The MT reading creates an anachronism. Deuteronomy 32:8 describes the division of the nations at Babel. Israel did not exist at Babel. Tying the apportionment of seventy nations to the number of Jacob's descendants who later went to Egypt imports a category that belongs to a later moment in redemptive history and severs the contrast the poem itself is making: the nations were allotted to the sons of God (v. 8), while YHWH retained Israel as his own portion (v. 9). That contrast requires a third party, the sons of God, who receive the nations. The MT's "sons of Israel" collapses that third party and makes v. 9 redundant.

The "sons of Israel" reading has no documented attestation before the second century AD. The second-century Greek versions (Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion) follow the MT, consistent with the broader pattern of rabbinic revision to eliminate divine council language that early Christians were using in Christological argument.

**Our rendering:** "sons of God"

**Footnote in translation (Deut 32:8):** "Heb. (DSS, 4QDeutj): *bene elohim*, 'sons of God.' The MT reads *bene yisrael*, 'sons of Israel,' an anachronism since Israel did not yet exist at Babel. The LXX renders 'angels of God,' an interpretive translation of the same underlying text the DSS preserves. The 'sons of Israel' reading has no documented attestation before the 2nd century AD; every known pre-2nd-century witness reads 'sons of God.' The 2nd-cent. Jewish Greek versions (Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion) all follow the MT."

### Genesis 46:27 and Exodus 1:5, Seventy or Seventy-Five

Both verses in the MT record that seventy members of Jacob's family went down to Egypt. The LXX reads seventy-five for both. The DSS supports seventy-five at Exodus 1:5. Acts 7:14, under inspiration, reads seventy-five.

The BSB follows the MT at Genesis 46:27 and Exodus 1:5 but follows Acts 7:14 at the NT citation of the same event, producing a numerical inconsistency within the base text. The AAB is committed to internal consistency, and Acts 7:14 is canonical text. We therefore adopt seventy-five at both Genesis 46:27 and Exodus 1:5 to align the OT text with its inspired NT citation.

The shift from seventy-five to seventy in the MT is likely connected to the broader second-century effort to tie the table of nations (Gen 10\) to the number of Jacob's family rather than to the seventy sons of God, collapsing the distinction between heavenly and earthly councils. Seventy-five breaks that equation.

**Our rendering:** seventy-five at both verses.

**Footnote in translation (Gen 46:27 and Exod 1:5):** "The LXX, DSS (Exod 1:5), and Acts 7:14 read seventy-five. The MT reads seventy. The 'seventy' reading has no documented attestation before the 2nd century AD; every known pre-2nd-century witness reads seventy-five. We follow the reading attested by the NT (Acts 7:14)."

### Noah's Tent (Genesis 9:21-22)

The Hebrew of Genesis 9:21 reads בְּתוֹךְ אָהֳלָהּ (*betokh oholah*), "inside her tent." The pronominal suffix is feminine. Most translations render this as "his tent," assuming a scribal error or reading the suffix as referring to Noah. We preserved the feminine suffix: "inside her tent."

The phrase "saw his father's nakedness" (Gen 9:22) is not merely visual. Leviticus 18:8 and 20:11 use "uncover the nakedness of your father" as a specific idiom for sexual relations with one's father's wife. Ham "saw his father's nakedness" in "her tent." The text implies that Ham violated Noah's wife. This explains why Canaan bears the curse: he may be the offspring of this act.

Noah's wife is not named in Scripture.[^4] The text's reticence invites the reader to discern what happened without spelling it out. We followed the Masoretic text as written.

**Footnote in translation:** "Heb. 'her tent'; traditionally rendered 'his tent.' See Lev 18:8."

## What This Translation Does Not Do

This section clarifies the limits of the project.

**It does not settle every interpretive question.** Many passages remain genuinely ambiguous. Where the Hebrew or Greek is unclear, we preserved that ambiguity rather than resolving it in favor of any particular tradition.

**It does not require belief in a single cosmological model.** Rendering *raqia* as "firmament" does not require readers to believe the sky is solid. It requires only that we translate what the Hebrew says and let readers engage with the text on its own terms.

**It does not override creedal Christianity.** Nothing in this translation contradicts the historic creeds. The corrections concern terminology and worldview categories, not the core doctrines of the faith.

**It does not replace teaching or preaching.** A translation is not a commentary. Study notes, sermons, and discipleship remain essential for understanding Scripture. This translation aims to give teachers and students better raw material to work with.

**It does not claim finality.** This is a public draft. We welcome correction, pushback, and refinement. The goal is fidelity to Scripture, not novelty for its own sake.

"Sin offering" imports post-biblical soteriology; "purification offering" reflects the Hebrew semantic range and ritual function per Milgrom's Anchor Bible Commentary.

**Rationale:** The אָשָׁם requires reparation for damage done. The offerer must repay what was taken plus a penalty, then bring the sacrifice. "Guilt offering" suggests psychological guilt; "restitution offering" reflects the actual function.

#### Atonement Cover (כַּפֹּרֶת / *kapporet*)

**BSB rendering:** "mercy seat" → **AAB rendering:** "atonement cover"

**23 verses:**

Exodus 25:17, 25:18, 25:19, 25:20, 25:21, 25:22, 26:34, 30:6, 31:7, 35:12, 37:6, 37:7, 37:8, 37:9, 39:35, 40:20

Numbers 7:89

1 Chronicles 28:11

Hebrews 9:5

**Rationale:** The כַּפֹּרֶת is the solid gold lid of the Ark where Yahweh's presence dwelt between the cherubim. "Atonement cover" renders the lexical meaning of the root כִּפֶּר (*kipper*, "to cover, purge, make atonement") directly. "Mercy seat" comes from Tyndale via Luther's *Gnadenstuhl*, a theological interpretation rather than a translation. The throne imagery associated with the Ark (1 Chr 28:2; Psa 99:5; 132:7) belongs to the footnote: every ANE deity's house contained a throne, and the Ark's position presupposes one above it, but that is interpretation of context, not the lexical meaning of כַּפֹּרֶת itself.

#### Forgiven (סָלַח / salach)

**BSB rendering:** "forgiven" → **AAB rendering:** "forgiven" (unchanged)

**Key Levitical occurrences:**

Leviticus 4:20, 4:26, 4:31, 4:35, 5:10, 5:13, 5:16, 5:18, 6:7

Numbers 15:25, 15:26, 15:28

**Rationale:** The Hebrew סָלַח (*salach*) means pardon or forgiveness and appears exclusively with God as subject throughout the Hebrew Bible. "Forgiven" is the accurate rendering and the AAB retains it unchanged. The question is not whether the word means forgiveness — it does — but what forgiveness means in this specific covenantal context. A footnote at the first occurrence (Lev 4:20) addresses this. The AAB notes *salach* here precisely because it is the word the author of Exodus 34:6-7 did not use when describing God's bearing of iniquity as a character attribute, choosing *nasa* instead. Preserving "forgiven" for *salach* and "bearing" for *nasa* maintains a distinction the biblical authors made with care.[^5]

But "forgiven" in this context means something precise that the English word can obscure. Three things define what *salach* does and does not mean here.

First, its scope is limited to unintentional sins (*shegagah*). Numbers 15:30-31 is explicit: the person who sins with a high hand, deliberately and defiantly, "blasphemes the LORD" and "shall be cut off." There is no sacrificial remedy for intentional rebellion. The Levitical system addresses inadvertent violations of covenant, not defiant ones.

Second, its mechanism is covering and bearing, not discharge. The priest performs *kipper* (the covering or purging of the sacred space from contamination), and *salach* follows as the declared outcome. The *salach* declaration rests on *kipper* and *nasa*, not on the cancellation of the underlying debt. The difference between managing a problem under forbearance and resolving it is what the system's own calendar reveals: Yom Kippur is required annually precisely because the regular sacrificial sequence, and the *salach* declarations that conclude it, do not achieve permanent resolution.

Third, *salach* appears exclusively with God as subject throughout the Hebrew Bible. It is never used of human forgiveness. This is the word the author of Exodus 34:6-7 did not use when describing God's bearing of iniquity as a character attribute, choosing *nasa* instead. The two words describe related but distinct acts: *nasa* is what God does in taking up the weight; *salach* is the covenant status that results. See the note on Exodus 34:6-7.

### The Accuser (הַשָּׂטָן / hasatan)

**BSB rendering:** "Satan" → **AAB rendering:** "the Accuser"

**14 verses (where the definite article appears):**

Job 1:6, 1:7, 1:8, 1:9, 1:12, 2:1, 2:2, 2:3, 2:4, 2:6, 2:7

**Note:** 1 Chronicles 21:1 retains "Satan" (no definite article, functioning as proper name).

**Rationale:** The Hebrew הַשָּׂטָן with the definite article is grammatically a title ("the adversary/accuser"), not a proper name. A faithful translation reflects the grammar of each passage. The same personal being is in view throughout Scripture; by NT times "Satan" functions as a proper name (John 8:44; 1 John 3:8; Rev 12:9). The linguistic shift from title to name does not represent theological development, only a change in how the language refers to the same entity.

#### Hell (γέεννα / Gehenna)

**Rationale:** A fully foreignizing translation would transliterate "Gehenna" here, as the AAB does with Sheol, Hades, Tartarus, and the Abyss. ### Specific Passages

#### Exodus 34:6-7 and Numbers 14:18 (The Divine Character Formula)

**BSB rendering (Exod 34:7):** "forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin... He punishes the children and their children for the sin of the fathers to the third and fourth generation."

**AAB rendering (Exod 34:7):** "bearing iniquity and transgression and sin... He visits the iniquity of the fathers upon the children and their children to the third and fourth generation."

**BSB rendering (Num 14:18):** "forgiving iniquity and transgression... He punishes the children for the sin of the fathers to the third and fourth generation."

**AAB rendering (Num 14:18):** "bearing iniquity and transgression... He visits the iniquity of the fathers upon the children to the third and fourth generation."

This formula is the most quoted theological formula in the Hebrew Bible, appearing in whole or part in Numbers 14:18, Psalms 86, 103, and 145, Joel 2:13, Jonah 4:2, Micah 7:18, Nahum 1:3, and Nehemiah 9:17. It is God's own self-disclosure at Sinai, spoken after the golden calf incident as his name is proclaimed. Every translation decision made here propagates through every downstream citation.

**nasa — "bearing," not "forgiving"**

The Hebrew verb נֹשֵׂא (*nasa*) means to lift, carry, or bear a physical load. It is not a word for forgiveness. Its semantic range covers carrying objects (Isa 46:7), bearing burdens (Exod 18:22), and carrying guilt where one party takes up what belongs to another (Lev 16:22; Isa 53:4, 11-12). The standard Hebrew word for divine pardon is סָלַח (*salach*), which appears exclusively with God as subject and is precisely what an author would reach for if forgiveness were the intended meaning. The author did not reach for it here.

The most important evidence is internal to Numbers 14 itself. In the verse immediately before Moses cites the formula, his explicit petition reads: "Pardon (*salach*) the iniquity of this people" (v.19). Moses uses the forgiveness word to name what he is asking for, then cites the formula which uses *nasa* instead. These are distinct acts in the same passage: *salach* is the outcome Moses requests; *nasa* is the character attribute grounding his confidence the request can be granted. The author had *salach* available and chose differently. Rendering both as "forgiving" collapses a distinction the text preserves with care.[^6]

The Leviticus 16 scapegoat passage uses *nasa* in its most concrete cultic form: the goat bears (*nasa*) all the iniquities of Israel into the wilderness. This is transference, not cancellation. The guilt does not cease to exist; it is carried out of the community's presence. Isaiah 53 deploys the same vocabulary at the height of prophetic theology: "he bore (*nasa*) the sin of many" (v.12). The Exodus 34 formula, the scapegoat ritual, and the Servant passage are structurally unified by this word. "Forgiving" severs these connections.

The rendering of *nasa* as "bearing" is the AAB's most significant departure from major modern translations, all of which render this word "forgiving" in this context. The case for "bearing" rests on the Hebrew alone.

**paqad — "visits," not "punishes"**

The Hebrew פֹּקֵד (*paqad*) denotes sovereign attentive action: a superior taking notice of and acting upon what is under his governance. Its semantic range covers attending to (Gen 21:1, where God "visits" Sarah and she conceives), mustering (Num 1:3), appointing (Jer 1:10), and reckoning with consequences (Exod 32:34). "Punishes" selects one end of this range and presents it as the whole. The AAB here follows the KJV, NKJV, ESV, NASB, and NRSV, all of which use "visiting" or "visits" against the NIV and NLT's "punishes."

The verse's internal asymmetry makes the error concrete. Mercy reaches a thousand generations; *paqad* is bounded to three or four, the span of a living household. The verse is structured as a declaration of the vastness of mercy, with *paqad* as a bounded counterweight that makes the mercy assertion credible rather than naive. "Punishes" inverts this structure: what is designed as a declaration of mercy becomes primarily a warning.

The rendering also creates an apparent conflict with Ezekiel 18:20, which states that the son will not bear the iniquity of the father. If Exodus 34:7 says God punishes children for their fathers' sins, these texts contradict each other. "Visits" dissolves the conflict: Exodus 34 describes how a household head's covenant failure shapes the conditions his descendants inherit, while Ezekiel 18 addresses individual judicial standing before God. These are different registers of the same reality. The translation creates the contradiction; the Hebrew does not.

The Numbers 14 narrative confirms the reading directly. The rebellious generation dies in the wilderness: *paqad* operates. The children inherit the land: they are not judicially penalized for their parents' rebellion. The text itself distinguishes between children living within conditions shaped by their parents' failure and children receiving a verdict for that failure (v.33). "Visits" makes the narrative outcome coherent; "punishes" makes it incoherent.

**avon — "iniquity" in both clauses**

The BSB renders עָוֺן (*avon*) correctly as "iniquity" in the first clause and then as "sin" in the second clause of the same verse. These are the same word. The three sin terms (*avon*, *pesha*, *chatta'ah*) are distinct and used with precision throughout the Hebrew Bible. Rendering the same word two different ways within the same liturgical formula is an internal inconsistency the AAB corrects by using "iniquity" in both clauses.

The distinction matters because bearing and forgiveness are not synonymous acts in the Levitical system. The mechanism words of that system are *nasa* and *kipper*, bearing and covering. The declared covenant outcome of the sacrificial sequence is *salach* (Lev 4:20, 4:26, 4:31, 5:10, and throughout), but the mechanism producing that declaration is not discharge of the underlying debt. It is the management of the covenant relationship through covering and bearing under God's sovereign forbearance.

The system's own structure reveals this limit. Yom Kippur is required annually. If the *salach* declarations of the regular sacrificial sequence achieved permanent resolution, annual repetition would be unnecessary. The Levitical calendar is the Hebrew system's own confession that it is managing a problem rather than resolving it.

The prophets confirm what the calendar implies. Jeremiah 31:34 presents the forgiveness of iniquity and the non-remembrance of sin as the distinctive feature of the new covenant: "I will forgive their iniquity and remember their sin no more." If the Mosaic system had been achieving this, there would be nothing new to announce. Isaiah 43:25 and 44:22 declare forgiveness from God's own initiative and character rather than as a description of what the sacrificial mechanism accomplishes. The promise of forgiveness is unambiguous throughout the Old Testament; what the canon does not yet disclose is the ground on which that promise can be permanently kept.

"Bearing" in Exodus 34:7 names the act accurately. God is not deceiving Israel when covenant status *salach* is declared after sacrifice. But the means by which bearing finally becomes discharge, the mechanism that permanently resolves what the Levitical system carried forward under forbearance, awaits the New Testament for its disclosure. Paul recognizes this in Romans 3:25, using the rare Greek πάρεσις (*paresis*, passing over or deferral) rather than ἄφεσις (*aphesis*, discharge or remission) to describe God's treatment of former sins. The sins of the old administration were not discharged; they were carried forward. What was carried forward is what the cross finally resolves.

#### 1 Corinthians 8:5–6 and 2 Thessalonians 2:4 (So-Called Gods)

**BSB rendering:** "so-called gods" → **AAB rendering:** "beings called gods" / "everything called a god"

**This is one of the most theologically significant translation errors in modern English Bibles.**

The Greek phrase is λεγόμενοι θεοί (*legomenoi theoi*), which means "what are called gods" or "those named gods." The participle λεγόμενοι is neutral; it simply indicates that these entities bear the designation "gods." It does not mean "falsely called" or "mistakenly named." Yet most English translations insert "so-called," a phrase that imports skepticism about the existence of these beings that Paul did not express.

**The Full Context of 1 Corinthians 8:4–6:**

"We know that an idol is nothing in the world, and that there is no God but one. For even if there are \[λεγόμενοι\] gods, whether in heaven or on earth (as indeed there are many gods and many lords), yet for us there is one God, the Father, from whom all things came and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom all things came and through whom we exist."

Notice what Paul actually says:

1. **"An idol is nothing"**: The physical statue has no inherent power  
2. **"There is no God but one"**: Yahweh alone is the supreme, uncreated God  
3. **"For even if there are gods... (as indeed there are many gods and many lords)"**: Paul explicitly affirms the existence of these beings  
4. **"Yet for us there is one God... and one Lord"**: These other "gods" are real but subordinate; believers owe allegiance only to the one God and one Lord

Paul's argument is not "these gods don't exist, so eat whatever you want." His argument is "these gods exist and are real spiritual powers, but they are not *your* gods. You belong to the one true God and the Lord Jesus Christ."

**Paul's Divine Council Theology:**

This reading aligns with Paul's statements elsewhere:

- **1 Corinthians 10:20–21:** "The sacrifices of pagans are offered to demons \[δαιμονίοις\], not to God, and I do not want you to be participants with demons. You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons; you cannot partake of the Lord's table and the table of demons."

Paul does not say "pagans sacrifice to nothing" or "demons don't exist." He warns that these are *real spiritual entities* with whom believers must not have fellowship. The danger is real precisely because the beings are real.

- **Ephesians 6:12:** "Our struggle is not against flesh and blood, but against the rulers, against the authorities, against the powers of this dark world, and against the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly realms."  
    
- **Colossians 2:15:** Christ "disarmed the rulers and authorities and made a public spectacle of them, triumphing over them by the cross."  
    
- **Romans 8:38–39:** "Neither angels nor rulers... nor powers... will be able to separate us from the love of God."

Paul consistently presupposes a populated spiritual realm with real beings, some loyal to God, some rebellious, over whom Christ has triumphed. The "gods" of the nations are not figments of imagination but spiritual powers that have been defeated and subordinated.

**Why "So-Called" Is Wrong:**

When translations render λεγόμενοι θεοί as "so-called gods," they:

1. **Add words not in the Greek.** The skeptical dismissal "so-called" is not a translation but an interpretation inserted into the text.  
     
2. **Contradict Paul's own argument.** The parenthetical "as indeed there are many gods and many lords" (ὥσπερ εἰσὶν θεοὶ πολλοὶ καὶ κύριοι πολλοί) explicitly affirms their existence. If Paul meant "these beings don't really exist," why would he add this affirmation?  
     
3. **Flatten the biblical worldview.** The Old Testament consistently presents other "gods" (אֱלֹהִים, *elohim*) as real spiritual beings, members of the divine council who rebelled or were assigned to the nations (Deut 32:8–9; Psalm 82; Dan 10:13, 20–21). Paul inherits this worldview. Translations that insert "so-called" import a later theological framework that denies the existence of these beings rather than affirming their subordination.  
     
4. **Undermine the force of Paul's monotheism.** Biblical monotheism is not the claim that no other spiritual beings exist. It is the claim that Yahweh alone is the uncreated, supreme God to whom all others owe allegiance and before whom all others will be judged (Psalm 82:1, 6–7). Paul's monotheism is robust precisely because it acknowledges real rivals whom Christ has defeated.

**The Translation:**

The AAB renders 1 Corinthians 8:5: "For even if there are beings called gods, whether in heaven or on earth (as there are many gods and many lords)..."

The participial phrase "what are called gods" preserves λεγόμενοι without either dropping the word or importing skepticism. It leaves open exactly what Paul leaves open: that these beings bear the designation "gods" and are real, while remaining subordinate to the one God.

**Footnote added (1 Cor 8:5):** "Gk. λεγόμενοι θεοί: 'what are called gods,' not 'so-called gods.' The skepticism implied by 'so-called' is not present in the Greek. Paul is subordinating these beings, not dismissing their existence. He makes their reality explicit in the same sentence ('as there are many gods and many lords') and elsewhere describes them as beings with whom believers must not have fellowship (1 Cor 10:20), as powers defeated by Christ at the cross (Col 2:15), and as rulers and authorities against whom the church still struggles (Eph 6:12). The biblical claim is not that these beings do not exist but that Yahweh alone is the uncreated God to whom all others owe allegiance (Ps 82:1, 6–8; Deut 32:8–9)."

**2 Thessalonians 2:4** presents the same issue. The man of lawlessness "opposes and exalts himself above everything called a god \[πάντα λεγόμενον θεόν\] or object of worship."

**AAB rendering:** "...above everything called a god or object of worship..."

The Antichrist figure sets himself above real spiritual powers, not above entities that do not exist. A figure exalting himself above beings who do not exist is no figure at all. The force of the passage depends on those powers being real.

**Footnote added (2 Thess 2:4):** "Gk. πάντα λεγόμενον θεόν: 'everything called a god,' same participial construction as 1 Corinthians 8:5. The man of lawlessness exalts himself above genuine spiritual powers, including the divine council members through whom nations are governed (Deut 32:8–9; Dan 10:13, 20–21). His claim to supremacy is blasphemous precisely because those powers are real. Compare Daniel 11:36–37."

This correction matters because it allows readers to encounter Paul's actual theology rather than a sanitized version that removes the spiritual warfare dimension of the New Testament.

#### 2 Thessalonians 1:9 (Destruction *from* the Lord's Presence)

**BSB/ESV rendering:** "They will suffer the punishment of eternal destruction, away from the presence of the Lord and from the glory of his might."

**NIV rendering:** "They will be punished with everlasting destruction and shut out from the presence of the Lord and from the glory of his might."

**AAB rendering:** "They will pay the penalty of eternal destruction from the presence of the Lord and from the glory of his strength."

**The issue:** The Greek preposition ἀπό followed by a genitive of source means "from," not "away from." The ESV's "away from" and the NIV's "shut out from" are not translations of ἀπό; they are translations of a theological conclusion about the nature of hell. The preposition carries no spatial sense of exile on its own. That sense has been inserted.

**The intertextual background:** The closest scriptural parallel is Isaiah 2:10, 19, 21, where the wicked hide from "the terror of the LORD and the glory of his majesty." The preposition there functions causally: the terror originates from the Lord and drives the wicked into the rocks. Paul is almost certainly drawing on this passage. Reading ἀπό causally \-- destruction proceeding from the Lord's presence \-- coheres with the Isaiah background and requires no supplemental spatial theology.

**Why the traditional rendering matters:** The "away from" translation has functioned as a proof text for hell as a place of geographic exile from God. That doctrine may or may not be true, but it cannot be read off this verse without importing what the preposition does not say. The AAB renders what Paul wrote. The theological debate belongs in the commentary, not the translation.

**Footnote added:** "Gk. ἀπό: 'from,' indicating source or origin. The traditional 'away from' is an interpretive expansion not required by the Greek. See Isaiah 2:10, 19, 21 for the probable background, where destruction proceeds from the Lord's presence rather than away from it."

#### Hebrews 6:4 (Ultimately Impossible)

**BSB rendering:** "impossible" → **AAB rendering:** "ultimately impossible"

The Greek ἀδύνατον means "impossible," and we preserve that sense. The author of Hebrews uses ἀδύνατον four times (6:4, 6:18, 10:4, 11:6), and in every instance the impossibility is genuine: it IS impossible for God to lie (6:18); it IS impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins (10:4); it IS impossible to please God without faith (11:6). We do not soften "impossible" to "difficult" here, as that would break the author's consistent usage.

However, Scripture demonstrates that impossibility operates in different senses. What is impossible for man is possible for God (Matt 19:26); it is impossible to serve two masters (Matt 6:24); one cannot love both God and money (Matt 6:24; Luke 16:13). These are ontological impossibilities defining the structure of reality, not statements about whether circumstances might ever change.

The impossibility in Hebrews 6:4 is likewise ontological. The author's argument throughout Hebrews centers on Christ's ἐφάπαξ (once-for-all) sacrifice (7:27; 9:12; 10:10). The impossibility in view is the impossibility of recrucifying Christ (v. 6), of demanding a second once-for-all offering, of belonging to Christ while simultaneously treating his blood as worthless.

This is NOT a statement that someone who doubts, struggles, or even walks away from faith cannot later genuinely repent. Christ is not recrucified each time someone believes; the prodigal returned; Peter was restored; the door remains open to all who turn back. The qualifier "ultimately" signals that the impossibility operates at the level of Christ's sufficient work and the either-or nature of covenant belonging, not at the level of individual human experience. The exhortation is to persevere, because there is no Plan B and no second sacrifice waiting if you treat Christ's finished work as nothing.

#### Hebrews 9:7 (Unintentional Sins)

**BSB rendering:** "sins committed in ignorance" → **AAB rendering:** "unintentional sins committed in ignorance"

Adding "unintentional" clarifies that the Day of Atonement sacrifices addressed שְׁגָגָה (inadvertent, unintentional sins), not high-handed rebellion. This aligns with the Levitical system and the author's argument.

#### Hebrews 9:10 (Time of Regeneration)

**BSB rendering:** "time of reform" → **AAB rendering:** "time of regeneration"

Greek διορθώσεως can mean "reform" or "new order/regeneration." Given the eschatological thrust of Hebrews and the new creation theology, "regeneration" captures the magnitude of change better than "reform."

#### Hebrews 12:15 (Falls Away)

**BSB rendering:** "falls short" → **AAB rendering:** "falls away"

Greek ὑστερῶν in this context carries the sense of apostasy, not merely missing a mark. The warning passage context (cf. Heb 6:4–6; 10:26–31) supports "falls away" as the intended meaning.

### Final Judgment Passages

These passages share a common problem: traditional rendering choices have settled questions the text itself leaves open. In most cases the settlement favors one theological tradition not because the source languages require it but because that tradition has dominated the translation history.

The AAB's goal here is not to take sides in the debate about final judgment. It is to draw the reader into what the text actually says. Where the Greek or Hebrew makes a distinction that popular reading has flattened, the footnote makes that distinction visible. Where an image has an intertextual history that shapes its meaning, the footnote traces it. Where a word has a consistent usage the reader may not be aware of, the footnote states it.

The reader who has only ever encountered one reading of these passages deserves to know the text is richer than they were shown. That is the whole goal. The translation does not resolve what the text does not resolve.

#### John 3:16 (Perish, Not Torment)

**Traditional rendering:** "...shall not perish but have eternal life." (ESV, NIV, BSB \-- consistent)

**AAB rendering:** Preserved.

**The issue:** The Greek verb is ἀπόλλυμι (*apollymi*): to destroy, kill, or be lost. It is not a torment word. The word has a consistent meaning across the entire NT with no exceptions. Herod seeks to apollymi the child (Matthew 2:13) \-- he means kill him. The lost sheep, lost coin, and lost son in Luke 15 are all apollymi: the opposite of being lost is being found, not escape from ongoing suffering. The word never describes conscious torment anywhere in scripture.

The contrast Jesus draws in this verse is therefore destruction paired against life, not torment paired against life. Popular reading has so thoroughly shaped the assumed meaning of "perish" that most readers do not notice what the text actually says. This is not a translation problem \-- the rendering "perish" is accurate \-- but the word's consistent meaning across scripture deserves to be made visible.

**Footnote added:** "Gk. apollymi: destroy, kill, be lost. The word never describes ongoing conscious suffering anywhere in the NT. Herod seeks to apollymi the child (Matt 2:13), meaning kill him. The lost sheep, coin, and son in Luke 15 are all apollymi \-- the opposite of being lost is being found, not escape from torment. The contrast here is between destruction and life, not torment and life. The text gives no signal that this is an exception to the word's uniform usage."

#### Isaiah 66:24 / Mark 9:43–48 (Unquenchable Fire and the Worm)

**Traditional rendering:** Mark 9:43: "the unquenchable fire" (ESV, NIV). Isaiah 66:24: "their worm shall not die, their fire shall not be quenched" (ESV).

**AAB rendering:** Preserved, with footnotes added at both locations.

**The issue:** Two distinct translation pressures cluster around this imagery. First, ἄσβεστος ("unquenchable") does not mean "never dies out." It means "cannot be extinguished from outside." The fire burns until its work is complete; the word says nothing about duration. Second, the verb κατακαίω in Matthew 3:12 and cognate passages means "burn up" or "reduce to ashes," a consummative destruction, not ongoing burning. The combination of ἄσβεστος and κατακαίω in the Baptist's preaching describes fire that cannot be stopped until it has fully consumed its object.

**The Isaiah background:** Jesus quotes Isaiah 66:24 three times in Mark 9 without adding interpretive commentary. The Isaiah passage describes corpses (Hebrew פְּגָרִים, *pěgarim*: carcasses, dead bodies) exposed to maggots and fire. The worm does not die and the fire is not quenched because nothing will prevent these bodies from being fully devoured. This is a statement about the completeness and unstoppability of judgment, not about the conscious ongoing experience of the dead.

**Footnote added (both locations):** "Heb. לֹא תִכְבֶּה / Gk. ἄσβεστος: 'cannot be quenched' or 'cannot be extinguished,' not 'burns without end.' The emphasis is on the unstoppability of the judgment, not its duration. The Isaiah 66:24 context describes exposed corpses, not living persons in torment. See also Ezekiel 20:47–48, where the same 'unquenchable' language describes a fire that nonetheless completes its destruction."

#### Daniel 12:2 (Shame and Everlasting Contempt)

**Traditional rendering:** "Some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt." (ESV, NIV similar)

**AAB rendering:** Preserved, with footnote added.

**The issue:** The Hebrew דֵּרָאוֹן (*dera'on*, "contempt" or "abhorrence") appears in only two places in the entire Old Testament: here and Isaiah 66:24, where it describes the reaction of the living to the exposed corpses of God's enemies. In Isaiah 66:24, the corpses are not experiencing abhorrence; they are causing it in those who look on them. The word denotes an emotion felt by observers, not by the dead. Reading "everlasting contempt" in Daniel 12:2 as the ongoing subjective experience of the risen wicked reads against the word's only other usage in the Hebrew Bible.

**The grammatical structure:** The verse places "shame" (בּוֹשֶׁת, *boshet*) and "everlasting contempt" in sequence but does not predicate both eternally. Shame describes what the risen wicked experience at judgment. Contempt describes how they are regarded forever by God and his people. The everlasting modifier attaches to the memory, not to the conscious experience.

**Footnote added:** "Heb. דֵּרָאוֹן (*dera'on*): the only other occurrence is Isaiah 66:24, where it describes the revulsion of the living toward exposed corpses, not the experience of the dead themselves. 'Everlasting contempt' most naturally refers to how the wicked are remembered forever rather than to their ongoing conscious state. Compare Isaiah 66:24."

#### Matthew 25:46 (Eternal Punishment / Eternal Life)

**Traditional rendering:** "And these will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life." (ESV, NIV similar)

**AAB rendering:** Preserved, with footnote added.

**The issue:** The Greek κόλασις (*kolasis*, "punishment") in the Septuagint and wider Greek literature frequently carries the sense of a death penalty or cutting off, not merely ongoing suffering. More significantly, the parallel structure with αἰώνιος ζωή ("eternal life") is theologically precise: the verse explicitly grants eternal life to the righteous but does not state that the wicked receive eternal existence. What is eternal is the punishment, not necessarily the punished.

**The adjective αἰώνιος:** In both parallel phrases αἰώνιος ("eternal" or "age-long") modifies the noun, not the subject's experience of it. Eternal life means life that lasts forever. Eternal punishment, on the same logic, means punishment whose effects are permanent and irreversible, not necessarily punishment consciously experienced forever. The translation does not settle this question; it should not be made to.

**Footnote added:** "Gk. κόλασις: punishment, penalty; sometimes specifically capital punishment in the Septuagint and Second Temple literature. The parallel with αἰώνιος ζωή ('eternal life') is deliberate: the righteous receive life that lasts forever. The text does not state that the wicked receive existence that lasts forever, only that their punishment is permanent and irreversible."

#### Revelation 14:9–11 (Smoke Rising Forever / No Rest Day or Night)

**Traditional rendering:** "The smoke of their torment goes up forever and ever, and they have no rest, day or night." (ESV, NIV similar)

**AAB rendering:** Preserved, with footnote added.

**The issue:** The identical imagery cluster \-- drinking God's wrath from a cup, torment in fire, smoke rising forever \-- reappears in Revelation 18:9–19:3 applied to Babylon, where the angel's interpretation is not ongoing conscious torment but total destruction: "she will be found no more" (18:21). The symbols converge in both passages. The interpretive key for one illuminates the other, and the text itself supplies the Babylon interpretation.

**The idiom εἰς αἰῶνας αἰώνων:** "Forever and ever" renders a Greek idiom of emphatic finality whose force is completeness and irreversibility. Its presence does not automatically require literal endlessness in every context; it communicates that the judgment is total and cannot be undone.

**The Isaiah 34 background:** The imagery cluster originates in Isaiah 34:9–10, where the same language \-- fire, sulfur, smoke rising forever \-- describes the destruction of Edom: "its smoke will ascend forever... from generation to generation it will lie desolate; no one will ever again pass through it." Edom is a place now ruined, not a population in ongoing conscious torment. "Forever rising smoke" is Isaiah's idiom for permanent, irreversible destruction \-- a monument of ruin. John's first readers, steeped in the prophets, would have heard Isaiah 34 behind Revelation 14 and 18–19 alike.

**Footnote added:** "The imagery here \-- cup of wrath, fire, rising smoke \-- originates in Isaiah 34:9–10, where the same language describes the permanent destruction of Edom ('no one will ever again pass through it'). It reappears in Revelation 18:9–19:3 applied to Babylon, where the explicit interpretation is destruction and permanent absence: 'she will be found no more' (18:21). The identical phrase 'smoke rises forever and ever' appears in 19:3 as the response to Babylon's fall. Read the Isaiah background and the Babylon parallel before drawing conclusions from this passage in isolation."

#### Revelation 20:10, 14–15 (The Lake of Fire as the Second Death)

**Traditional rendering:** "They will be tormented day and night forever and ever...This is the second death, the lake of fire." (ESV, NIV similar)

**AAB rendering:** Preserved, with footnote added.

**The issue:** The text itself supplies its interpretive gloss: "This is the second death, the lake of fire" (v.14). Throughout prophetic and apocalyptic literature, when a visionary symbol is identified as something, that identification interprets the symbol \-- it does not merely label it. The seven fat cows are seven years; the tree is the king; the lake of fire is the second death. The question the text presses is therefore not what happens in the vision but what "second death" means.

**Second death in the Targums:** The phrase does not appear in the canonical Old Testament but occurs in the Aramaic Targums, early Jewish translations of the Hebrew scriptures drawing on oral traditions predating the New Testament. In every occurrence it means dying a second time, the literal death of the whole person. This is the conceptual world John's first readers would have brought to the phrase.

**Death and Hades thrown in:** The passage also casts Death and Hades \-- personified as the fourth horseman and his companion in Revelation 6 \-- into the lake of fire. Their fate is then interpreted by 21:4: "death shall be no more," drawing on Isaiah 25:8 and 1 Corinthians 15:26. If the lake of fire symbolizes the annihilation of Death as a cosmic power, this shapes how we read the fate of everything else the vision throws into it.

**Footnote added:** "The phrase 'second death' appears in the Aramaic Targums, where it consistently means dying a second time, the death of the whole person. John's identification of the lake of fire as the second death (v.14) follows the interpretive pattern of apocalyptic literature: the vision symbolizes the reality named, not the reverse. Compare the fate of Death and Hades here with 21:4 ('death shall be no more') and Isaiah 25:8. The lake of fire is the symbol; the second death is the interpretation the text itself supplies."

### Nephesh/Psychē Corrections

These corrections address passages where "soul" language actively imports Greek dualism or creates internal inconsistency. See "A Note on Greek Dualism and the Biblical Worldview" above for the rationale.

#### Ezekiel 18:4, 20 (The One Who Sins)

**BSB rendering:** "The soul who sins" → **AAB rendering:** "The one who sins"

Hebrew *nephesh* here means "the person," not "the immortal soul." This verse is constantly misread as teaching about eternal destiny when Ezekiel's point is about individual mortality and responsibility. Persons die; there is no separate soul-component being addressed.

#### Matthew 16:26 / Mark 8:36–37 (Life, Not Soul)

**BSB rendering:** "forfeits his soul" → **AAB rendering:** "forfeits his life"

Greek *psychē* in Mark 8:35 is rendered "life" ("whoever loses his *life* for my sake"). The very next verses (8:36–37) use the identical Greek word but are typically rendered "soul." This internal inconsistency makes no sense. We render *psychē* as "life" consistently throughout the passage.

#### Deuteronomy 6:5 (Footnote at the Shema)

**Footnote added:** "Heb. *nephesh*. Often translated 'soul,' nephesh does not refer to an immaterial component separable from the body (a Greek concept foreign to Hebrew thought). Nephesh means the whole person: one's life, self, being, vitality, appetite. To love God with all your nephesh is to love Him with your whole life."

#### Matthew 10:28 (Footnote on Body/Soul)

**Footnote added:** "Gk. *psychē*. Jesus contrasts what humans can do (kill the body) with what God alone can do (destroy both body and psychē in Gehenna). This does not teach Platonic dualism (an immortal soul trapped in a disposable body). Rather, it affirms God's ultimate authority over the whole person."

#### 1 Thessalonians 5:23 (Footnote on Spirit/Soul/Body)

**Footnote added:** "This tripartite language ('spirit, soul, and body') expresses totality, like 'heart, soul, mind, and strength' in Deut. 6:5the Shema. Paul prays for the whole person to be sanctified, not three separable components."

#### Ecclesiastes: הֶבֶל Rendered "Vapor" Throughout

The Hebrew הֶבֶל (*hebel*) literally means breath, mist, or vapor: something real and visible for a moment but impossible to grasp or hold. Traditional English translations render it "vanity" (KJV, ESV) or "meaningless" (NIV) or "futility" (BSB), all of which are interpretive abstractions foreign to the word itself. The AAB renders it "vapor" throughout Ecclesiastes to preserve the concrete image the author chose.

This rendering allows the book's two great refrains to work together as the author intended: vapor and chasing the wind are both atmospheric phenomena — things you can see but cannot hold. "Vanity," "meaningless," and "futility" each import a theological or emotional interpretation. "Vapor" leaves the image intact and lets the reader supply the application, which is what a wisdom text invites.

**All occurrences of הֶבֶל in Ecclesiastes are rendered "vapor."** The opening and closing refrain ("vapor of vapors, everything is vapor," 1:2; 12:8) sets the frame. Every individual verdict ("this too is vapor") echoes it.

#### Ecclesiastes 2:8: שִׁדָּה וְשִׁדּוֹת Rendered "Musical Instruments"

The Hebrew שִׁדָּה וְשִׁדּוֹת (*shiddah wəshiddot*) is a hapax legomenon — appearing nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible — whose meaning was already disputed in antiquity. Ancient translations diverge: the LXX and Aramaic Targum point toward service and hospitality roles; Babylonian rabbinic tradition reads male and female demons; Palestinian tradition reads carriages; other rabbinic sources treat it as a type of ornate chest. Isaac ibn Ghiyath (d. 1089), the first rabbinic commentator on Qohelet, surveyed all these traditions and concluded the best interpretation is a wondrous musical instrument that existed in the time of the Kingdom and was lost when the First Temple was destroyed (b. Sotah 48b). The Arabic cognate root *shada* carries the sense of something good, pleasant, and desirable. Modern scholarship has proposed "concubines" based on a Hebrew cognate connection, but this reading has the weakest support from the ancient witness tradition and conflicts with the contextual coherence of the list, where every other item is a lawful dominion-oriented acquisition. The AAB renders "musical instruments" as the reading best supported by the ancient evidence while acknowledging the term remains uncertain.

#### 1 Chronicles 21:1 (Satan Without the Article)

**Footnote added:** "Heb. *śāṭān* without the definite article. In Job and Zechariah, *haśśāṭān* (with article) is rendered 'the Accuser' as a title describing a function. Here the anarthrous form may function as a proper name."

This is related to the Accuser/Satan distinction but placed here because the footnote clarifies a transitional case.

### Pauline Anthropological Terminology

Modern translations often obscure Paul's cosmic-conflict worldview by rendering key Greek terms in ways that flatten his theology into individual moral psychology. We corrected three categories of mistranslation and adopted capitalization conventions that make Paul's personification of Sin and Death visible.

#### Old Man / New Man (ἄνθρωπος)

Modern translations render παλαιὸς ἄνθρωπος as "old self" and καινὸς/νέος ἄνθρωπος as "new self." We restored "old man" and "new man" because:

1. ἄνθρωπος means "man," not "self"  
2. The context is corporate: the old man is Adamic humanity under Satan's headship; the new man is the body of Christ  
3. Ephesians 2:15 uses identical language ("one new man") and BSB preserves "man" there because the corporate meaning is undeniable; consistency requires "man" throughout  
4. Colossians 3:10 connects to Adam typology ("renewed in knowledge after the image of its Creator")

#### Romans 6:7: δικαιόω as "Justified"

BSB renders δεδικαίωται ἀπὸ τῆς ἁμαρτίας as "freed from sin." We restored "justified from Sin" because:

#### Sin and Death Capitalized

In Romans 5-8 and 1 Corinthians 15, Paul personifies Sin (ἁμαρτία) and Death (θάνατος) as cosmic powers that invaded God's world, enslaved humanity, and rule as rival lords. We capitalize "Sin" and "Death" when Paul uses personification language to make this visible in English.

**Scholarly support:**

- Bruce Longenecker (Baylor): "It is not uncommon now to capitalise 'sin' in the singular to denote a cosmological independent entity seemingly with intentionality and agency... a superhuman force."  
- Martinus de Boer (VU Amsterdam): "Paul's personification of sin as an external enslaving evil power characterises his use of 'Sin' in the singular... Sin came into the human world as an alien force conquering territory that belonged to God."

**Criteria for capitalization:**

Capitalize "Sin" when: Sin is subject of active/agentive verbs (entered, reigned, seized, deceived, killed, dwells); Sin is described as master/lord with dominion over slaves; Sin is parallel to Death as ruling power.

Capitalize "Death" when: Death is subject of ruling verbs (reigned, has dominion); Death is paired with Sin as co-ruler; Death is described as enemy to be destroyed.

Do NOT capitalize when: referring to plural sins/transgressions (ἁμαρτίαι); referring to physical death as an event; general ethical usage outside personification contexts.

#### Study Note on σάρξ (Sarx)

We retained "flesh" throughout for σάρξ rather than context-specific substitutions (e.g., "world," "family," "human nature"). This decision requires explanation, since Colin Hamer's review identified distinct semantic domains that could warrant different English renderings.

**The Four Senses of σάρξ / בָּשָׂר**

Paul and the biblical authors use σάρξ (Greek) and בָּשָׂר (Hebrew, *basar*) with four distinct but related senses:

**1\. Physical flesh/tissue:** the material substance of bodies

| Reference | Context |
| :---- | :---- |
| 1 Cor 15:39 | "Not all flesh is the same: there is one flesh of humans, another flesh of animals, another flesh of birds, another of fish" |
| 1 Cor 15:50 | "Flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God" |
| John 1:14 | "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us" |

This is the most concrete sense: physical meat, muscle, bodily tissue. No theological loading; simply the stuff bodies are made of.

**2\. Family/lineage:** kinship, shared ancestry, covenant bond

| Reference | Context | Note |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Gen 2:24 | "They shall become one flesh" | Hebrew *basar* here means family union, not mere physical contact |
| Matt 19:5-6 | "The two will become one flesh... they are no longer two but one flesh" | Jesus quotes Genesis; sarx \= covenant kinship |
| Mark 10:8 | "The two will become one flesh" | Same usage |
| Phil 3:3-4 | "We... put no confidence in the flesh. If anyone else thinks he has reason for confidence in the flesh, I have more" | Paul then lists his *family credentials*: circumcised, tribe of Benjamin, Hebrew of Hebrews |
| Rom 9:3 | "My brothers, my kinsmen according to the flesh" | Flesh \= ethnic/family relationship |

Colin's comment on Gen 2:24: "*Basar* is certainly family." The "one flesh" of marriage is not primarily about sexual union but about the creation of a new kinship unit; a new household. This is why adultery is such a severe covenant violation: it ruptures family bonds, not merely physical ones.

**3\. Domain of the fallen world:** life lived under Satan's rule, apart from God's Spirit

| Reference | Context | Note |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Rom 7:5 | "When we were living in the flesh, our sinful passions... were at work in our members" | Flesh \= the old domain |
| Rom 8:4-5 | "Who walk not according to the flesh but according to the Spirit. For those who live according to the flesh set their minds on things of the flesh" | Flesh vs. Spirit as two competing lordships |
| Rom 8:8-9 | "Those who are in the flesh cannot please God. You, however, are not in the flesh but in the Spirit" | Locative sense: "in the flesh" \= under its domain |
| Gal 5:16-17 | "Walk by the Spirit, and you will not gratify the desires of the flesh. For the flesh desires what is contrary to the Spirit" | Flesh and Spirit as opposing powers |
| 1 Cor 3:1 | "I could not address you as spiritual people, but as people of the flesh" | Flesh \= worldly orientation |
| John 8:15 | "You judge according to the flesh" | Flesh \= worldly/satanic perspective |

Colin's comment on Rom 7:5 and 8:4-9: "Sarx \= the world," meaning Satan's world-system. This is not merely "sinful human nature" in an abstract sense but life lived under the wrong lord, in the wrong household. The flesh/Spirit contrast maps onto the two-households framework: those "in the flesh" belong to Satan's domain; those "in the Spirit" belong to Christ's.

**4\. Human frailty:** mortal weakness, vulnerability, creaturely limitation

| Reference | Context | Note |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Rom 8:3 | "God... sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh" | Christ took on human frailty (not sinfulness) |
| 2 Cor 4:11 | "So that the life of Jesus may be manifested in our mortal flesh" | Flesh \= mortal, vulnerable body |
| Gal 4:13-14 | "Because of a bodily ailment I preached the gospel to you... though my condition was a trial to you, you did not scorn or despise me" | Flesh \= physical weakness |

Colin's comment on Rom 8:3: This is "human frailty," distinct from the domain sense. Christ entered human mortality and weakness without entering Satan's domain. The incarnation involved taking on sense 4 (frailty) without sense 3 (allegiance to the fallen world).

**Why We Retained "Flesh" Throughout**

Given these distinct senses, why not translate contextually (e.g., "family" in Gen 2:24, "world" in Rom 8:5, "human nature" in Rom 8:3)?

1. **Paul deliberately uses one word across contexts.** The semantic overlap is theologically significant. Fallen humanity (*flesh* as frailty) belongs to the wrong household (*flesh* as family) under the wrong lord (*flesh* as domain). Using different English words would obscure Paul's deliberate wordplay and the conceptual connections he draws.  
     
2. **The marriage-to-domain connection is essential.** When Paul says believers have "died to the law through the body of Christ" so they may "belong to another" (Rom 7:4), he is using marriage/family language (*flesh*) to describe transfer between domains (*flesh*). Translating "flesh" as "family" in one place and "world" in another hides this connection.  
     
3. **Readers can learn the semantic range.** Rather than making interpretive decisions for readers, we preserve the source-language term and explain its range in study notes. This respects readers' intelligence and allows them to trace the word across Scripture.  
     
4. **Context disambiguates.** In most cases, the immediate context makes clear which sense is operative. "One flesh" in a marriage context obviously means kinship union. "Walk according to the flesh" in a Spirit-contrast obviously means the fallen domain. Skilled readers navigate this naturally.

**The Study Note**

At key passages (Rom 7:5; 8:3-9; Gal 5:16-24), the AAB includes a study note:

*Gk. sarx, "flesh." Paul uses this word with several distinct senses: (1) physical flesh/tissue, (2) family/kinship, (3) the domain of the fallen world under Satan's rule, and (4) human frailty/mortality. Here the context indicates sense \[X\]. The connection between senses is theologically significant: fallen humanity (flesh as mortality) belongs to the wrong household (flesh as family) until transferred to Christ's household through death and resurrection.*

This approach honors both the source language and the reader: we translate what Paul wrote while equipping readers to understand what he meant.

### Internal Consistency Comparison

One mark of a reliable translation is rendering the same source-language term consistently. Here is how the three translations compare:

| Source Term | AAB | ESV | NIV |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| שְׁאוֹל (Sheol, 60+x) | Sheol (100%) | Sheol / grave / pit (mixed) | grave / realm of dead / death (rarely Sheol) |
| ᾅדης (Hades, 10x) | Hades (100%) | Hades 6x / hell 4x | Hades 2x / hell 8x |
| γέεννα (Gehenna, 12x) | hell (100%, footnoted as Gehenna) | hell (100%) | hell (100%) |
| אֲבַדּוֹן (Abaddon, 7x) | Abaddon (100%) | Abaddon 2x / Destruction 5x | Abaddon 1x / various 6x |
| נֶפֶשׁ (nephesh, 750+x) | varies by context | soul / life / person (inconsistent) | soul / life / you / throat (inconsistent) |
| ψυχή (psychē, 100+x) | life (where dualism implied) | soul / life (inconsistent even within pericopes) | soul / life (inconsistent) |
| חַטָּאת (chattat, 114x) | purification offering (100%) | sin offering (100%) | sin offering (100%) |
| נֹשֵׂא (*nasa*, Exod 34:7; Num 14:18; Isa 53 etc.) | bearing (100%) | forgiving / bore (inconsistent by context) | forgiving / bore (inconsistent by context) |
| סָלַח (*salach*, 10+ Levitical uses) | forgiven (100%) | forgiven (100%) | forgiven (100%) |

**The ESV's internal inconsistency is particularly notable** because it markets itself as "essentially literal." A translation claiming word-for-word fidelity should not render the same Greek term differently based on theological context. Yet the ESV translates ψυχή as "life" in Mark 8:35 but "soul" in the very next verses (8:36-37). Same word, same pericope, different translation. This is not literalism; it is interpretation disguised as translation.

### Overall Assessment

**The ESV** is well-crafted with elegant prose and broad acceptance. But it perpetuates translation choices from Tyndale (1530s) without revisiting whether they reflect the Hebrew and Greek. More troubling, it is internally inconsistent: Hades/hell, Abaddon/Destruction, ψυχή as "life" then "soul" in adjacent verses. A translation claiming to be "essentially literal" should not render the same term differently based on theological comfort.

**The NIV** prioritizes readability and sometimes captures meaning better than the ESV (e.g., "vault" for רָקִיעַ is closer than "expanse"). But it frequently interprets rather than translates: "sons of Israel" in Deut 32:8 follows the inferior MT against DSS/LXX evidence; "angels" for בְּנֵי הָאֱלֹהִים in Job 1:6 is interpretation, not translation; "one new humanity" in Eph 2:15 imports modern categories into Paul's ἄνθρωπος.

**The AAB** is a set of targeted corrections to the BSB, not a from-scratch translation. We lack the resources and committee breadth of the ESV or NIV. But we are trying to do what those translations have not: incorporate post-1947 scholarship, maintain internal consistency, and translate what the source languages say rather than what tradition expects.

## Scholarly Endorsements

The AAB is a public draft. We are actively seeking review, critique, and endorsement from scholars across relevant disciplines: biblical Hebrew and Greek, textual criticism, Ancient Near Eastern studies, Second Temple Judaism, New Testament theology, and translation theory.

We do not want uncritical praise. We want honest engagement. This section exists to record that engagement publicly, in whatever form it takes.

### How Endorsements Work

Endorsements here are not binary. We recognize three forms:

**Full endorsement.** The endorser has reviewed the AAB rationale and translation choices and affirms the overall project, its hermeneutical commitments, and its translation decisions as more consistent and transparent than current major alternatives. Disagreements with specific renderings, if any, are noted inline.

**Domain endorsement.** The endorser is a specialist in a particular area (e.g., Levitical sacrifice, divine council, NT anthropology) and has reviewed the AAB's treatment of that domain. Their endorsement applies to that domain only; they are not certifying areas they have not reviewed.

**Endorsement with noted disagreement.** The endorser affirms the project's goals and overall approach but disagrees with one or more specific translation choices. Their disagreement is recorded here with their reasoning, and we treat it as an open item under review. This is not a disqualification; it is exactly the kind of engagement we need.

All three forms are equally welcome. A domain endorsement from a specialist who has only examined Part 2 is more valuable to us than a vague full endorsement from someone who has only skimmed the document. Precision matters. Caveats are a feature, not a liability.

We will not alter endorsers' stated positions or caveats without their written consent. If a later version of the AAB changes a rendering they disagreed with, we will note that in their entry.
