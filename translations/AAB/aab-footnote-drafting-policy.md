# AAB Footnote Drafting Policy

A footnote explains the text to an ordinary reader and grounds every claim it makes in Scripture. The rules below govern how AAB footnotes are written.

## 1. Every proposition links to its key passage in Scripture

This is the governing rule. A footnote may not assert a theological claim it does not anchor in a passage. Each proposition carries, in parentheses, the passage or passages it rests on, so the reader can check it. Bare assertion is not allowed: if a claim cannot be tied to Scripture, it does not belong in the note. Link the claim to the strongest passages, not to all of them. So "Satan holds people captive" carries Luke 4:6 and Heb 2:14-15, and "every person belongs to God" carries Ezek 18:4 and Ps 24:1.

## 2. State the positive, not the negative

Say what a thing is. Do not set a claim up by denying its opposite, and never deny a position no one in view holds. Carry the point with the positive instead, and the denial is heard without being spoken. "Jesus breaks the hold and brings the prisoners home to God" tells the reader nothing was paid to the captor without the word "not." "God made every person, so each one belongs to him" settles ownership without saying Satan does not own them.

## 3. Explain; do not hedge

A footnote explains the text to a reader, not a thesis to scholars. Drop the academic hedges: that a reading is the framework's, that it is not the consensus, that the verse does not quite say it. State the reading plainly and let the linked passages carry the warrant. The honesty is in the passages cited, not in a disclaimer.

## 4. Write at an eighth-grade reading level

An eighth-grade reader should understand the note on the first pass. Use common words. Replace technical pairs and Greek- or Latin-derived abstractions with plain English. "Custody, not ownership" becomes "Satan holds them like a jailer holds a prisoner; God made them, so they are his."

## 5. Name things plainly, with no in-house shorthand

Name the subject directly. Write "Satan," never terms like "the rival" that are defined only in the papers and mean nothing to a reader. Do not gesture at a thing you intend to assert: say it.

## 6. Carry an abstract relation with a concrete picture

Where a relation is abstract, give the reader a picture already in hand. The jailer and the prisoner carry captivity without ownership. The kinsman-redeemer, the relative with the right to buy a family member back, carries the ransom relation. The picture does the work the abstract pair cannot.

## 7. Keep the text's own figure, and add to it rather than overwrite it

Build on the figure the passage itself uses. Romans 7:4 uses the law of marriage, where a wife is bound to her husband until his death. Keep that figure and lay the transfer over it as a second layer; do not replace Paul's husband with Satan. The note serves the text the reader is looking at before it serves the system behind it.

## 8. Put ongoing realities in the present and standing tense, bounded by Christ

State a condition that still holds in the present, as a standing reality bounded by Christ, not in the past. Reserve the past tense for what is finished, such as a particular group's completed transfer. "Satan holds all who are under his power, like a jailer, until Christ frees them," not "Satan held them," which reads as already over for everyone.

## 9. Explain the chosen rendering, and reintroduce no rejected reading

Explain the rendering the translation chose and its basis. Do not bring back a reading the translation has deliberately moved away from, and do not import a mechanism no passage states. The Romans 3:25 note explains hilasterion as the place of purgation and names propitiation nowhere, because the translation left it; no ransom note makes Satan a seller who is paid, because no passage activates that.

## 10. On a contested rendering, name the common reading and give the reason the AAB chose otherwise

Where the AAB renders a contested word or phrase against a rendering most readers know from other translations, the note says so and gives the reason for the AAB's choice. A reader who meets an unfamiliar rendering should be able to see that a choice was made and why, rather than take it for an error. State the reason as the positive textual basis for the AAB's reading, not as an apology for departing. Two limits keep this from undoing rules 3 and 9. The note carries a reason and never a bare floating alternative, so it informs instead of hedging. And it marks a difference in translation, not a rejected theology: where the common reading's content is a mechanism the text does not carry, the AAB renders cleanly and does not platform it. So the first Day of Purification note can say the day is commonly called the Day of Atonement and that the AAB renders it otherwise because the day's work is purging the house (Lev 16:16-19) and "atonement" is a later English word laid over kipper, whose sense is to purge; while the Romans 3:25 note explains "place of purgation" and names propitiation nowhere, because that rendering would carry a wrath-appeasement the text does not.

## 11. Keep the registers distinct

The marriage register and the ransom register are different and stay separate. Marriage is a bond to a husband that only death dissolves. Ransom is captivity to a jailer that rescue breaks. A note in one register does not borrow the mechanics of the other.

## 12. Hold the settled doctrinal lines, each on its passages

Some lines are settled and every relevant note must hold them, each carrying its own basis:

- God holds title to every person by creation, so no one is anyone else's possession (Ezek 18:4; Ps 24:1).
- Satan holds by permitted power, as a jailer holds prisoners, not by title (Luke 4:6; Job 1:12; Heb 2:14-15).
- The rescue breaks the hold and is rendered to no one; the captor is overpowered and stripped, not paid (Col 2:15; Matt 12:29; Luke 11:21-22).
- The captives are won back for God, who is the destination of the purchase (Rev 5:9; Acts 20:28).
- The death is shared, not a swap that lets the believer skip it: one died for all, therefore all died (2 Cor 5:14; 1 Cor 15:22).

## 13. Ground in Scripture and the words of the text, not in later authority

Ground every proposition in Scripture and, where it helps a reader, in the plain sense of the original-language words. Do not rest a reader-facing claim on later theologians or church history. Constructive support is the canon and, at the furthest reach, the witness of the first two centuries.

## 14. Gloss original-language terms plainly, and cross-link a word across its uses

Give an original-language term transliterated, with a plain gloss of its sense, as in "Gk. lytron, the price paid to set a captive free." Where the same word is rendered differently in different places, cross-link the uses so the reader sees one word under two aspects: hilasterion is the throne seat as an object (Heb 9:5) and the place of purgation as the function Paul applies (Rom 3:25).

## 15. Be brief, and make every citation earn its place

Keep notes short. Cite the two to four strongest passages for a point, not an exhaustive chain. Every citation must do work the others do not.

## 16. Keep the apparatus consistent across notes

When the same proposition appears in more than one note, state it the same way and at the same reading level each time. Uneven notes, plain in one place and technical in the next, read as carelessness, and a contested claim retyped from memory across several notes carries the same slip into all of them. State it once with care and reuse that wording.

## 17. Formatting

Use no em dashes except inside a direct quotation. Use no horizontal dividers.
